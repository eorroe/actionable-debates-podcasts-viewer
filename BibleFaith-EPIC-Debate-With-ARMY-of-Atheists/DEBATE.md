# Cliffe Knechtle's EPIC Debate With ARMY of Atheists

## Overview

Christian apologist Cliffe Knechtle engages in a fiery, open-air campus debate with a large group of atheist and secular students who challenge his presence, his worldview, and the truth claims of Christianity. The discussion covers the value of human life, the nature of God, the historicity of Jesus, biblical oppression, sexuality, moral relativism, Buddhism, and transgender/intersex identity. The debate exists because the atheist students view his preaching as intrusive and hateful, while Knechtle views it as a necessary exercise of free speech and intellectual honesty on a college campus.

## When to Follow Debate

- When studying value-based objections to theism in secular campus settings
- When addressing the claim that Christianity is inherently oppressive (racism, slavery, colonialism, sexism)
- When discussing moral relativism versus objective morality in public debate
- When exploring the purpose and design of human sexuality from a Christian perspective
- When comparing Christianity with Buddhism and other non-theistic worldviews
- When addressing transgender, intersex, and gender identity questions against a Christian worldview
- When examining whether Jesus is a historical figure or myth
- When responding to claims that the Bible justifies slavery, racism, or oppression of women
- When discussing how Christians should engage hostile secular audiences
- When preparing to defend the resurrection of Jesus Christ in hostile environments

## Glossary / Prerequisite Terms To Know Before Following Debate

- **Apologetics**: The discipline of defending religious doctrines through systematic argumentation and evidence
  - **Christian Apologetics**: Specifically defending the truth claims of Christianity
  - **Presuppositional Apologetics**: Arguing from foundational assumptions about God, truth, and morality
- **Moral Relativism**: The belief that moral judgments are true or false only relative to some particular standpoint (e.g., culture, individual) and that no standpoint is uniquely privileged
  - **Cultural Relativism**: Morality is determined by one's culture
  - **Individual Relativism**: Morality is determined by personal preference
- **Objective Morality**: The belief that moral truths exist independently of human opinion, culture, or preference
  - **Natural Law**: Moral law existing above and beyond human law; requires God as its source
- **Resurrection**: The Christian claim that Jesus Christ rose bodily from the dead three days after his crucifixion
  - **Historicity**: The quality of being based on or concerned with historical events rather than myth or legend
- **Intersex**: A person born with physical sex characteristics (chromosomes, gonads, genitals) that do not fit typical binary notions of male or female bodies
  - **Androgynous**: Identifying or expressing both masculine and feminine qualities; neither clearly male nor female
  - **Transgender**: A person whose gender identity differs from the sex they were assigned at birth
- **Siddhartha Gautama Buddha**: The historical founder of Buddhism; taught the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path as a solution to suffering
  - **Nirvana**: The Buddhist goal of liberation from suffering and the cycle of rebirth; described by a participant as "true death"
  - **Eightfold Path**: The Buddhist path to end suffering through right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness, and concentration
  - **Reincarnation**: The Hindu and Buddhist belief that the soul is reborn into a new body after death
- **Good Samaritan**: The parable of the Good Samaritan found in Luke 10; Jesus uses it to directly attack racism by showing that a despised foreigner was the true neighbor
- **Golden Retriever Example**: Knechtle's hypothetical example used to demonstrate that without God and objective morality, there is no basis to say having sex with a dog is wrong
- **Mark Twain Quote**: "It is not the parts of the Bible I do not understand that disturb me, it is the parts I do understand" - used by an atheist participant to criticize the Bible's moral content
- **Patriarchy**: A social system in which men hold primary power and predominate in roles of political leadership, moral authority, social privilege, and control of property
- **Genesis 2:24**: Biblical passage stating "a man shall leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh" - cited as defining God's purpose for sexuality and marriage
- **Polygamy**: The practice of having multiple spouses simultaneously
  - **Mistress**: A woman who has a continuing sexual relationship with a married man
  - **Greco-Roman Sexuality**: Ancient Mediterranean sexual norms that were often exploitative and accepted practices like pederasty and prostitution
- **Canaanite Religion**: Ancient Near Eastern religion characterized by practices including child sacrifice and ritual prostitution
- **Mark of Cain**: A biblical reference to the mark God put on Cain after he murdered Abel; misrepresented by a participant as being used to justify anti-Black racism
- **Constantine**: Roman Emperor who converted to Christianity and merged Pagan values with Christian holidays for political unity
- **Romans Chapter 1, 1 Corinthians Chapter 6, 1 Timothy Chapter 1**: Biblical passages cited by Knechtle as communicating that homosexual practice is wrong
- **fornication**: Sexual relations between people not married to each other
  - **Adultery**: Voluntary sexual relations between a married person and a person who is not their spouse
- **Paganism**: Polytheistic or non-Abrahamic religious traditions; used here to describe pre-Christian European and African religions
- **Sermon on the Mount**: Jesus' ethical teachings found in Matthew chapters 5-7, including "love your neighbor" and "turn the other cheek"
- **Isaiah 53**: The "Suffering Servant" prophecy describing a figure who suffers for the sins of others
- **Nuremberg Trials (1945-1949)**: Post-WWII trials of Nazi war criminals; Robert Jackson established "there is a law above the law"
- **Civil Rights / Birmingham Jail**: Reference to Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail," where he appealed to God's law above unjust human law

## Discussed Topics

### Topic 1 / View 1

#### Examples

##### Example 1: "Why is woman valuable?"

An atheist student challenges Cliffe Knechtle directly, asking "Why is woman valuable?" She accuses him of sexism and demands that he justify the value of women without reference to the Bible. She states her own view: "I'm a citizen of the world and my purpose is to do good."

##### Example 2: Intersex and Androgynous Identity

A participant identifies as intersex and androgynous, stating "I don't identify with myself as a woman. I am a trans sex person. I identify with myself as androgynous. I'm not a woman. I'm intersex." When asked about their value, they respond: "for my ability to create and to change the world to make it a better place."

##### Example 3: Value as Image Bearers

Knechtle counters that human value comes from being "a precious human being created in the image of God" and that "God loves you." He argues that all humans have equal value because they are all created in the image of God, regardless of race, sex, or condition.

#### Best Practices

- Address the question of human value directly rather than deflecting
- Acknowledge that Christianity has a real history of oppressing women and use that to show that such oppression contradicts Christ's actual teachings
- Distinguish between the misuse of Christianity and the teachings of Jesus Christ himself
- Use the parable of the Good Samaritan to demonstrate that Jesus directly confronted racism and tribalism
- When engaging someone who identifies as intersex or transgender, treat them with genuine respect while explaining your theological position
- Acknowledge that all humans have value regardless of whether you agree with their worldview
- Be consistent: if you affirm the value of all humans, do so on the basis of a coherent foundation

#### Keep In Mind

- Atheists have their own burden of justification: if there is no God, on what basis is a human being "valuable"?
- Evolutionary materialism ("evolved to a higher order") does not inherently provide a basis for intrinsic human value
- The question "Why is woman valuable?" forces the Christian to articulate why every human being has value
- If value is merely functional (ability to create, change the world), then those who cannot do those things have less value - a dangerous slope
- Intersex and transgender identities are real human experiences that must be engaged with compassion
- Christianity has a genuine historical problem with racism, sexism, and colonialism that must be acknowledged before the gospel can be heard

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not dismiss or mock gender identity questions; engage them seriously
- Do not use gender identity as a wedge to dehumanize the person asking the question
- Acknowledge the real harm done by Christians who have oppressed women, people of color, and LGBTQ+ people
- Reject any form of white supremacy, racism, or sexism as "totally contradicting Jesus Christ"

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Responding to "Why is woman valuable?" with a Bible quote without explaining the underlying philosophy of human dignity
  **Solution:** Explain that humans have value because they are made in God's image, not merely because of utility
- **Problem:** Ignoring the historical oppression of women and minorities by Christians
  **Solution:** Acknowledge the real sins of the church, distinguish them from Christ's teachings, and call them what they are: evil
- **Problem:** Dismissing intersex or transgender identity as unimportant
  **Solution:** Engage the person with respect, affirm their humanity, and gently explain the theological basis for biological sex as God's design

### Topic 2 / View 1

#### Examples

##### Example 1: "I am God"

An atheist student declares "I believe that I am God. I am my own God." When asked what this means, they explain: "It means that I create my own reality and I manifest what I want to see in this world."

##### Example 2: Refusal to engage

The same student says: "I am creating my own reality right here. I'm saying your discussion is stupid and it shouldn't go on. It's pointless." They use their "own reality" claim as a shield to avoid substantive engagement.

##### Example 3: All gods exist

A student claiming to follow multiple religious traditions states they believe in "the African Gods, the Celtic gods, the Buddhist gods and the Hindu gods" and insists "all of them" exist. They say they have "no problem with Jesus" but do not want to hear about him on campus.

#### Best Practices

- Gently point out the logical inconsistency of claiming to be God while needing to eat, sleep, and depend on others
- Distinguish between "creating your own reality" as a philosophical claim and the actual reality we all share
- Challenge the "all gods exist" position by asking for the logical basis of contradictory truth claims
- Maintain intellectual honesty and patience even when the other person is not engaging in good faith
- Point out that respecting someone's right to speak is different from endorsing their views
- Use examples of shared reality (gravity, suffering, mortality) to show that some things are not subject to individual creation
- Show that the claim "I am God" or "I create my own reality" is a way to avoid accountability

#### Keep In Mind

- "I create my own reality" is often a philosophical shield against having one's views challenged
- If everyone creates their own reality, rational discourse is impossible
- Believing in all gods simultaneously is logically incoherent because many gods make contradictory truth claims
- The fact that someone "doesn't want to hear about Jesus" is not a philosophical objection; it is a preference
- True tolerance requires allowing views you disagree with to be expressed; silencing others is not tolerance
- The Bible teaches that humans are created IN God's image, not that they ARE God

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not ridicule or mock someone for claiming to be God; engage the claim philosophically
- Do not escalate hostility even when the other person is insulting or dismissive
- Maintain free speech principles: you have the right to speak, and so do they
- Do not call campus security or threaten removal unless actual disruption occurs
- Recognize that hostile audience members may be testing boundaries rather than seeking genuine dialogue

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Taking "I create my own reality" as a sincere philosophical position and trying to refute it seriously
  **Solution:** Recognize it as a rhetorical shield; point out that shared reality makes conversation possible
- **Problem:** Getting angry at disrespectful behavior and responding in kind
  **Solution:** Maintain composure; Knechtle's patience is one of his greatest strengths
- **Problem:** Being driven off campus by a hostile crowd
  **Solution:** Stand firm on free speech grounds while remaining charitable

### Topic 3 / View 1

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Jesus as Myth

An atheist participant states "I think he's a story like any other story. Good story, bad story. I mean I guess it's a pretty good story." They compare Jesus to Greek mythological figures like Apollo, noting "the story of the Son of God Rising" is a common motif in ancient mythology.

##### Example 2: Constantine and Political Christianity

The same participant claims Christianity "actually has a lot of elements of older Pagan religions involved in it that were messed together as a political movement" and references Constantine merging Pagan and Christian practices for political unity.

##### Example 3: Black Magic and the Nazis

The participant calls Jesus "a practitioner of black magic" and bizarrely claims "the Nazis were experimenting with" resurrection and black magic, suggesting Jesus' resurrection is comparable to occult experimentation.

#### Best Practices

- Distinguish between mythological stories (gods who rise from the dead in fiction) and historical claims about Jesus
- Acknowledge that some aspects of Christianity were shaped by political forces (e.g., Constantine), but argue this does not negate the truth of Jesus' resurrection
- Be familiar with common mythological parallels (Osiris, Mithras, Dionysus, etc.) and have ready responses
- Emphasize that Jesus' resurrection is a historical claim about a specific person in a specific time and place, not a generic myth
- Note that the disciples' transformation and willingness to die for their belief is historically significant
- Distinguish between "Christianity" (the institution) and "Jesus Christ" (the person); agree that much of Christian history has been corrupt

#### Keep In Mind

- The resurrection is the central historical claim of Christianity; if it is false, Christianity is false
- Jesus is not a myth in the same category as Apollo or Zeus - he is claimed to be a historical person who walked in first-century Palestine
- The disciples' transformation from fearful cowards to bold proclaimers of the resurrection requires historical explanation
- Mythological resurrection stories typically involve gods who are not historical persons; Jesus is claimed to be both God AND a historical person
- Constantine lived in the 4th century AD, roughly 300 years after Jesus; his political use of Christianity does not address whether Jesus rose from the dead
- Mark Twain's quote "It's not the parts of the Bible I don't understand that disturb me, it's the parts I do understand" is an emotional reaction, not an argument

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not dismiss the questioner as unintelligent for seeing Jesus as myth
- Do not conflate the atrocities of Christianity (Crusades, Inquisition, colonialism) with the person of Jesus Christ
- Be honest about where Christianity has failed; this builds credibility
- Avoid name-calling or condescension when someone makes outlandish claims (Nazis and black magic)

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Responding to mythological parallels by simply quoting the Bible
  **Solution:** Engage the historical evidence for Jesus' resurrection specifically
- **Problem:** Defending all of Christian history when the questioner is attacking genuine corruption
  **Solution:** Distinguish between Jesus Christ and Christianity the institution; agree that Christianity has often betrayed Christ
- **Problem:** Getting drawn into defending Constantine or the Crusades
  **Solution:** Acknowledge the evil, separate it from Christ's teachings, and redirect to the resurrection

- The Ethiopian eunuch (Acts 8) was one of the first converts to Christianity, showing Christianity's early presence in Africa
- Christianity has been a force for liberation as well as oppression (e.g., abolition movement, civil rights)

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not dismiss or minimize the historical trauma of slavery, colonialism, and racism
- Do not tell a Black person they are "brainwashed" for being Christian
- Do not defend or justify slavery, racism, or colonialism
- Acknowledge that the white Christians who oppressed Black ancestors were acting against Christ's teachings

### Topic 5 / View 1

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Golden Retriever Hypothetical

Knechtle asks: "If there is no God and if morality is relative, then what is the basis for saying that me having sex with my golden retriever is wrong?" He argues that without objective moral standards, moral judgments become mere subjective preference.

##### Example 2: Three Reasons for Sex

Knechtle articulates three purposes for sex: (1) companionship replacing loneliness through lifelong commitment, (2) physical pleasure as a gift from God, and (3) bringing children into the world. He insists the context for this is marriage between a man and a woman.

##### Example 3: Greco-Roman and Canaanite Sexuality

A participant asks how Knechtle explains ancient societies with "polygamous sexuality" where men had wives, mistresses, and sex with other men, referencing Greco-Roman and Canaanite practices. Knechtle agrees these were wrong and contrasts them with biblical design.

##### Example 4: Homosexuality and Brainwashing

A participant claims the Bible is "used by the patriarchy to suppress women and other marginalized groups." Knechtle agrees the Bible has been used for oppression but argues Christ himself is different and calls for equal value for all people.

##### Example 5: Overpopulation and Gay Adoption

A participant asks about overpopulation and argues that homosexual couples could adopt children. Knechtle responds: "Never once did I say the purpose of sex is to have babies" and clarifies that companionship and pleasure are also purposes.

##### Example 6: Sex Change Operations

A participant asks whether Knechtle would support a sex change operation if it allowed someone to be with the person they love. Knechtle says he doesn't think anyone has to have a sex change operation and asserts the purpose of sexuality is for a male and female to make a lifelong commitment.

#### Best Practices

- Address the purpose of sex biblically without being crude or dismissive
- Clarify that you do not condemn homosexuals as people while explaining why you believe homosexual practice is contrary to God's design
- Distinguish between loving the person and disagreeing with the practice
- Be honest about all purposes of sex (companionship, pleasure, procreation) without reducing sex to only one purpose
- Acknowledge the real question of overpopulation without endorsing eugenic or utilitarian reasoning
- Use Romans 1, 1 Corinthians 6, and 1 Timothy 1 as biblical references for the traditional view
- Do not claim to support gay marriage while believing it is wrong; be consistent
- Be prepared for misrepresentation and calmly correct it

#### Keep In Mind

- Sexual orientation is a deeply personal and often painful issue; engage with compassion
- The golden retriever example is meant to expose the logical problem of moral relativism, not to equate homosexuality with bestiality
- If there is no God and no objective morality, there is no basis to condemn any sexual behavior - including heterosexual adultery, fornication, or bestiality
- The purpose of sex in Christian theology includes companionship, pleasure, and procreation within a male-female marriage
- The fact that some people experience same-sex attraction does not mean God approves of all expressions of that attraction
- Many people who experience same-sex attraction live celibate, faithful Christian lives
- The church has often failed to speak with compassion and clarity on sexuality

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Do not equate homosexuality with bestiality or pedophilia; the golden retriever example is about moral relativism, not about gay people
- Do not hate or condemn homosexuals; they are image-bearers of God with equal value
- Do not compare the homosexuality debate to slavery in a way that dismisses the real trauma of slavery
