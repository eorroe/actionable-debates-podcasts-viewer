# Conservative Christian vs 20 Liberal Christians – Marriage, Abortion, Empathy, and Progressivism

## Overview

This debate features conservative Christian commentator Allie Beth Stuckey, author of "Toxic Empathy: How Progressives Exploit Christian Compassion," engaging with 20 liberal Christians on four foundational claims: that the Bible defines marriage exclusively as one man and one woman, that abortion is a grave moral evil, that empathy can be toxic and lead to sin, and that progressivism and Christianity are at odds. The discussion takes place in Dallas, Texas, with 13 participants identifying themselves by name and speaking on one or more of the four topics: Gilbert (a gay Christian man raised in a conservative home), pastors Danny and John, Jared, Steve, Katrina, Angie, Shane, Joseph, Colby, Tim, Kyle, and Paul. Jaden (a member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints) speaks only on the topic of empathy. Six additional participants speak in shorter exchanges without identifying themselves by name during the portions of the debate captured in the transcript, bringing the total to 20.

## When to Follow Debate

- When preparing to discuss Christian sexual ethics, marriage definitions, or LGBTQ+ inclusion in church contexts
- When analyzing the theological and biblical arguments around abortion policy from a Christian framework
- When exploring how Christians should understand the relationship between empathy, love, and moral truth-telling
- When a user asks about the compatibility of progressive political or social positions with historic Christian doctrine

## Glossary / Prerequisite Terms To Know Before Following Debate

- **Marriage (Conservative View)**: An exclusively monogamous, heterosexual union between one man and one woman, rooted in Genesis 1:27, reiterated in Matthew 19:4-5, and defined as an eternal reality reflecting Christ's relationship to the Church in Ephesians 5
- **Marriage (Liberal View)**: A covenantal, loving union that may include same-sex couples, understood as an archetypal rather than literal creation ordinance, with context and cultural setting determining biblical application
- **Prescription vs. Description (Biblical Interpretation)**: The distinction between biblical texts that command or approve behavior (prescription) versus those that merely report what occurred (description); conservatives emphasize prescription for defining holy marriage, liberals note extensive descriptive polygamy in scripture
- **Personhood**: The status of being a human person with moral worth; conservatives locate it at conception (Psalm 139, Jeremiah 1:5), liberals locate it at viability, first breath (Jewish tradition, Exodus 21), or visual development of features
- **Common Grace**: The conservative theological concept that God bestows general goodness, kindness, and moral character even on non-Christians, explaining why LGBTQ+ individuals may exhibit the fruit of the Spirit
- **Toxic Empathy**: Allie Beth Stuckey's term for the misuse of empathy that leads to affirming sin or destructive behavior, defined as feeling so deeply what another feels that one validates lies or harmful choices (1 Corinthians 13:6: love "rejoices with the truth")
- **Empathy (Liberal View)**: The biblical practice of entering into another's feelings (Romans 12:15: "Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep"), seen as the necessary starting point for Christian love
- **Progressivism (Stuckey's Definition)**: The belief that morality and truth evolve with culture and human experience, applied to sexuality, gender, economics, and justice
- **Progressivism (Liberal View)**: A posture of growing in moral understanding over time, akin to Jesus' progressive challenge to second-temple Judaism, trusting that the Holy Spirit leads the church into greater justice
- **Creation Ordinance**: A divine command established at creation; Stuckey argues monogamous heterosexual marriage is a creation ordinance reaffirmed throughout scripture, while liberals argue Genesis should be read as archetypal mythology
- **Natural Law**: The conservative appeal to biological complementarity (male and female reproductive systems designed to join) as evidence for heterosexual marriage; liberals reject this as insufficient, noting celibacy and infertility
- **Arsenokoitai**: The Greek term in 1 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10 traditionally translated as "homosexuality"; liberal participants argue it refers to economic exploitation, pederasty, or cultic prostitution, not consensual adult same-sex relationships
- **Kadeshim**: The Hebrew term for male cultic prostitutes referenced in the Old Testament; participants debate whether Leviticus 18:22 condemns all same-sex relations or only cultic/exploitative practices
- **John 14:6**: The verse "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father except through me," cited by Gilbert as agreement with Stuckey on the exclusivity of Christ
- **Exodus 21:22-25**: The passage describing a fine for causing a miscarriage in a fight between men, cited by Angie as evidence that biblical personhood did not extend to the unborn
- **Numbers 5:11-31**: The "sotah" ritual for a suspected adulteress, cited by Katrina as potentially involving induced miscarriage and suggesting a biblical hierarchy of sins
- **Southern Strategy**: The mid-20th century Republican political realignment strategy; Steve cites it to argue the pro-life movement's origins were political rather than theological
- **Bob Jones University**: The fundamentalist Christian university whose founder used biblical frameworks to defend racial segregation; Joseph invokes it as a cautionary parallel to conservative biblical interpretation on sexuality
- **Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints**: Participant Jaden's denomination; he identifies as a member of the LDS Church and expresses belief in pre-mortal spirit gender, which the other participants note is distinct from orthodox Christian theology

## Discussed Topics

### Topic 1 / View 1 – Biblical Marriage: One Man, One Woman (Conservative / Allie Beth Stuckey)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Ephesians 5 and the Bride of Christ

Stuckey argues that marriage is not merely a social contract but an earthly reflection of an eternal reality: the marriage between Christ (the groom) and the Church (the bride). In Ephesians 5, Paul writes that marriage reflects this relationship, and because the Church is the singular collective bride, only one man and one woman can reflect this eternal pattern. She cites Revelation 20 as the final chapter of the Bible showing this marriage, framing the entire biblical narrative from Genesis 1 to Revelation 20 around monogamous heterosexual marriage.

##### Example 2: Matthew 19:4-5 and Jesus' Citation of Genesis

When the Pharisees tested Jesus on divorce in Matthew 19, Stuckey notes that Jesus did not merely cite the Mosaic law but went "all the way back to the creation ordinance," quoting Genesis on male and female. She argues Jesus' citation demonstrates that he took Genesis seriously as literal and normative for marriage definition, not merely as a cultural artifact.

##### Example 3: Celibacy as a Valid Calling

When Gilbert (a gay Christian man) asks whether gay people must be celibate, Stuckey affirms that all Christians are called to deny fleshly desire and take up their cross (Matthew 16:24). She distinguishes between being gay as an identity and experiencing same-sex attraction, referencing Rosaria Butterfield, Christopher Yuan, and Becket Cook as examples of Christians who live celibately while acknowledging ongoing same-sex attraction.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Anchor marriage definition in the full canonical arc: Genesis 1:27, Matthew 19:4-5, Ephesians 5, and Revelation 20
- ✅ Distinguish prescription (what God commands) from description (what the Bible reports about fallen people)
- ✅ Apply consistently: if polygamy in the Bible is descriptive rather than prescriptive, the same hermeneutic should apply to all biblical narratives
- ✅ Affirm celibacy as a legitimate Christian calling for those called away from marriage (Matthew 19:12)
- ✅ Address the objection from Genesis 1 population growth by noting the text is about creation order, not merely reproductive mechanics

#### Keep In Mind

- The conservative argument does not deny that polygamy occurred in biblical history; it claims only monogamous heterosexual marriage is *prescribed* as holy
- Stuckey's framework requires reading Genesis as literal history, which liberal participants directly challenge by citing Jewish scholarly tradition reading Genesis as archetypal
- Gilbert, a gay Christian raised in a conservative home, represents a significant constituency within Christianity who experience same-sex attraction and affirm same-sex marriage, citing an "affirming movement" of Christians who love and revere the Bible but read it as condemning only exploitative same-sex practices

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Framing same-sex attraction as a "disordered desire" or "sinful orientation" can cause severe psychological harm to LGBTQ+ Christians, including the statistic cited by Gilbert that gay kids from religious homes are 8 to 12 times more likely to commit suicide than gay kids from secular or affirming households (a figure Gilbert cites from research on LGBTQ+ youth in evangelical and conservative Christian settings; the wide range of 8–12× reflects different studies using varying methodologies and definitions of "religious home," with some studies finding lower multipliers and others finding higher ones, but all showing dramatically elevated risk)
- The ex-gay movement's historical failures and harms should be acknowledged; Stuckey references Rosaria Butterfield and Christopher Yuan without addressing the movement's broader critique
- Claiming that being gay is inherently linked to pedophilia or pederasty (as participants Tim and Jared briefly touched on when discussing the Greek terms arsenokoitai and kadeshim) is a harmful stereotype not supported by contemporary scholarship

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Citing David and Solomon's multiple wives as evidence the Bible condones polygamy
  **Solution:** Clarify the prescriptive/descriptive distinction; David's adultery with Bathsheba and Solomon's 700 wives are presented as causes of trouble, not models to follow
- **Problem:** Arguing Genesis 1 requires vegetarianism because it prescribes eating plants
  **Solution:** Note that Genesis 1:27 (male and female) is a creation *order* statement about identity, while Genesis 1:29 (diet) is a pre-fall provision superseded after the flood (Genesis 9:3)
- **Problem:** Assuming all who disagree with same-sex marriage are motivated by hatred or political animus
  **Solution:** Engage the actual theological argument about creation, Christology, and the nature of marriage as an eternal sign

### Topic 1 / View X – Biblical Marriage: Contextual and Inclusive (Liberal Christians)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: David, Solomon, and Jacob's Multiple Wives

Tim and other participants cite David (multiple wives), Solomon (700 wives), Jacob (married sisters Leah and Rachel), and Lamech (two wives, Genesis 4:19) as biblical examples of polygamy that are never explicitly condemned by God in a direct prohibition verse (i.e., there is no verse that says "you shall not have multiple wives"); they argue that if Stuckey's hermeneutic (creation order + Jesus' citation of Genesis) were applied consistently, these passages should also be rejected as defining marriage, yet they are accepted as part of biblical history.

##### Example 2: Genesis as Archetypal, Not Literal

Pastor Danny cites his practicing rabbi friends—whom he describes as rabbis across the Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist Jewish denominations—who would not read Genesis 1-2 as a literal scientific account of creation but as an archetypal story about human relationships and the "helper" concept. He argues the "helper" (ezer) in Genesis 2:18 refers not exclusively to a wife but to a collaborative relationship, and that reading Genesis literally is a Protestant innovation, not a Jewish or church fathers' position.

##### Example 3: Gilbert's Lived Experience as a Gay Christian

Gilbert, raised in a conservative Christian home, argues that the church's condemnation of same-sex relationships causes profound suffering: gay kids from religious homes are 8 to 12 times more likely to commit suicide. He cites an "affirming movement" of Christians—including organizations such as The Reformation Project, Q Christian Fellowship, and individual theologians like Matthew Vines and Justin Lee—who love and revere the Bible but read it as condemning only exploitative (pederastic, economic, or coercive) same-sex practices, idolatrous (cultic or idol-worship-related) practices, or non-committed (promiscuous or casual) same-sex practices, not monogamous covenanted relationships. He notes Romans 1 describes people abandoning natural relations due to idolatry and lust, not consensual committed love.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between the Bible's descriptive reporting of polygamy and its prescriptive ideal
- ✅ Read Genesis 1-2 as archetypal narrative rather than scientific or legal code
- ✅ Consider the cultural context of New Testament terms (arsenokoitai, kadeshim) in Greco-Roman practices of pederasty, exploitation, and cultic prostitution
- ✅ Center the experience of LGBTQ+ Christians as theological data, not merely a social justice talking point
- ✅ Note that Jesus' citation of Genesis in Matthew 19 answers a question about divorce, not a comprehensive marriage theology

#### Keep In Mind

- Liberal participants do not deny that some biblical texts speak against same-sex behavior; they argue the modern category of "homosexuality" (coined in 1868 by Karl-Maria Kertbeny, added to English Bible translations—specifically the Revised Standard Version—in 1946) does not map onto ancient practices
- The argument that Genesis should not be read literally is grounded in Jewish interpretive tradition, not merely modern secularism
- Gilbert affirms John 14:6 (Jesus as the only way to salvation), demonstrating that affirming same-sex relationships is not inherently tied to theological liberalism on Christology

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Celibacy mandates for gay Christians without providing community, relationship, and meaningful connection contribute to the elevated suicide risk Gilbert cites
- Using the term "same-sex attraction" rather than "gay" can be experienced as erasing identity and denying the fullness of a person's experience
- The ex-gay movement's history of harm should be acknowledged when invoking Rosaria Butterfield or Christopher Yuan as exemplars

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming liberal Christians reject biblical authority
  **Solution:** Note Danny's statement that "the Bible is not a parlor trick" and his commitment to reading scripture in its total context
- **Problem:** Claiming Jesus definitively took Genesis literally
  **Solution:** Jesus' citation of Genesis in Matthew 19 addresses a specific question about divorce; he does not provide a comprehensive hermeneutic for how to read Genesis 1-2
- **Problem:** Reading Romans 1 as a blanket condemnation of all same-sex relationships
  **Solution:** Paul's argument in Romans 1:18-32 is widely understood by scholars as a rhetorical trap for Jewish moralists (Romans 2:1), describing lust-driven idolatrous practices, not committed covenanted love

### Topic 2 / View 1 – Abortion as a Grave Moral Evil (Conservative / Allie Beth Stuckey)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Intentional Killing Framework

Stuckey opens this section by defining abortion as "the intentional killing of a human being in the womb." She secures agreement on the species (human) and the act (killing), then asks whether killing an innocent human is always evil. When participant Jared refuses to call abortion murder, Stuckey draws a parallel to Matthew 5:28 (lust as adultery in the heart) and Matthew 5:22 (anger as murder in the heart), arguing that intent is not the sole determinant of moral evil—abortion kills an innocent person regardless of the pregnant woman's emotional state.

##### Example 2: Psalm 139 and Jeremiah 1:5 on Personhood

Stuckey cites Psalm 139:13-16 ("For you knit me together in my mother's womb") and Jeremiah 1:5 ("Before I formed you in the womb I knew you") as proof that God attributes personhood, intentionality, and moral worth to the unborn. She counters the "first breath" argument by noting that ventilator-dependent people are not thereby stripped of personhood, and that fetuses practice breathing motions in utero, breathing amniotic fluid.

##### Example 3: The Pain Receptacle Argument

Steve argues that because most abortions occur before 10 weeks gestation, before pain receptors develop, characterizing abortion as "violent and painful" is misleading. Stuckey counters that pain is not the standard for murder: "Are you saying that there are a lot of people who are murdered who can't feel it? Are you saying that murder is justified because they can't feel it?" She characterizes the abortion pill as "starving that human being of the nutrients that he or she needs to survive."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Define terms clearly at the outset: abortion as intentional killing of a human in the womb
- ✅ Use scriptural evidence of God's personal formation of the unborn (Psalm 139, Jeremiah 1:5)
- ✅ Apply the moral logic consistently: if killing is wrong based on innocence and humanity rather than pain, then gestational age does not negate personhood
- ✅ Address the rape/incest argument directly: Stuckey notes these constitute less than 1% of abortions (approximately 0.5-0.9% according to Guttmacher Institute data from 2018-2019, though the exact source year is not specified in the transcript)
- ✅ Distinguish between accidental miscarriage (Exodus 21:22, a fine) and intentional abortion

#### Keep In Mind

- Stuckey's argument assumes personhood at conception based on biblical and biological continuity; liberal participants reject this based on developmental markers or breath
- The "less than 1% rape/incest" statistic is contested; Stuckey uses it to argue that most abortions are elective
- Steve's pain receptor argument represents a common moderate position; Stuckey rejects it as morally insufficient

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Framing abortion as "murder" without distinction from other forms of killing can inflame rhetoric and contribute to political violence
- Ignoring the bodily autonomy argument and the woman's personhood in the abortion discussion reduces the moral complexity
- Katrina cites a statistic that 75% of juveniles in jail come from low-income situations (households below the federal poverty line) where abortion was unavailable; she does not provide a specific source or study, and the figure should be treated as a claim made in the debate rather than a verified statistic

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Conceding that abortion is healthcare without defining what healthcare excludes killing
  **Solution:** Ask for a coherent definition of healthcare that includes intentional killing of a class of human beings
- **Problem:** Using Numbers 5 (the sotah ritual) to argue the Bible condones abortion
  **Solution:** Note the ritual's ambiguity; Stuckey and liberals disagree on whether it describes miscarriage or a curse of barrenness/leg pain; most scholars do not read it as condoning intentional abortion
- **Problem:** Citing Exodus 21:22 as proof the Bible treats fetal death as a mere property fine
  **Solution:** The passage describes accidental harm in a fight, not intentional killing; compare to manslaughter vs. murder distinctions in modern law

### Topic 2 / View X – Abortion as Healthcare and Moral Complexity (Liberal Christians)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Personhood at First Breath and Developmental Stages

Jared and Katrina argue that Jewish tradition locates the beginning of life at first breath (Genesis 2:7, God breathing into Adam), not conception. Katrina notes that when the majority of abortions occur (first 6 weeks), the fetus has not developed pain receptors (earliest at 10 weeks), questioning whether "violent and painful" is accurate. She states she personally has "reservations against abortion" but opposes using personal religious convictions to legislate for non-Christians.

##### Example 2: Exodus 21:22-25 and Fetal Value

Angie cites Exodus 21:22-25, where causing a miscarriage in a fight results only in a fine, while harming the woman requires lex talionis (life for life, eye for eye). She argues this passage demonstrates that the Old Testament did not treat the unborn as persons with equal value to born women. Stuckey counters that the passage describes accidental harm (manslaughter), not intentional killing (murder).

##### Example 3: Socioeconomic Justice and Access

Katrina connects abortion access to systemic injustice, noting that abortion disproportionately affects low-income women, and that 75% of juveniles in jail come from situations where women felt they could not give life a fair chance (a statistic she cites in the debate without providing a specific source). She argues that the conservative focus on abortion while ignoring born children reveals a hierarchy of concern.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Acknowledge the genuine moral complexity and acknowledge personal reservations
- ✅ Use Exodus 21:22-25 and Jewish interpretive tradition to challenge the personhood-at-conception framework
- ✅ Distinguish between personal religious conviction and public policy in a pluralistic society
- ✅ Note developmental science on pain reception and fetal development without reducing personhood to pain
- ✅ Cite the rape/incest exception to challenge blanket prohibitions

#### Keep In Mind

- Liberal participants are not uniformly pro-choice; Katrina describes herself as having personal reservations against abortion while supporting legal access
- The argument from Numbers 5 is controversial even among liberal scholars; the Hebrew "thigh falls away" is ambiguous and may refer to a curse of barrenness rather than miscarriage
- The historical argument that the religious right adopted abortion as a political issue after the Southern Strategy failed on segregation (cited by Steve) is a contested historical claim

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Denying personhood to the unborn based on developmental stage or location can be experienced as dehumanizing by pro-life advocates
- Using the bodily autonomy argument without addressing fetal humanity can appear to ignore half the moral equation
- Failing to acknowledge the psychological impact of abortion on some women can undermine credibility

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Conceding that abortion is always killing without defending why that killing is justified
  **Solution:** Clarify the moral framework: bodily autonomy, personhood thresholds, or the lesser-of-two-evils principle
- **Problem:** Citing Numbers 5 as clear biblical support for abortion
  **Solution:** Acknowledge the interpretive ambiguity; most Jewish and Christian scholars do not read Numbers 5 as a prescription for abortion
- **Problem:** Ignoring the conservative argument from Psalm 139 as merely "poetic"
  **Solution:** Engage the theological weight of biblical passages about God's intimate knowledge of the unborn, even if one disagrees with the conclusion

### Topic 3 / View 1 – Empathy Can Be Toxic and Lead to Sin (Conservative / Allie Beth Stuckey)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Affirming Transgender Identity as Toxic Empathy

Stuckey gives a specific example: "If I feel so deeply that someone who sits across from me says you know what I was assigned male at birth but I am trapped in the wrong body... feeling so deeply how that person feels that you get to the point of affirming their identity and saying yes." She argues this is toxic empathy because it affirms "a lie that you can be born in the wrong body," which is "destructive for the individual." She grounds this in Genesis 1:27: "God created us male and female. We don't see any other category."

##### Example 2: Abortion Empathy and the Forgotten Child

Stuckey extends the empathy argument to abortion: "when we have so much strong empathy for the woman, her plight, what she's going through, and we say, 'I have to support her right to choose because I feel her plight.' What we've done there is we've ignored the humanity and the need and the rights of that child inside the womb. We have sacrificed on the altar of empathy, a living human being."

##### Example 3: Love as the Endpoint, Not Empathy

Stuckey distinguishes between empathy and love: "I think that you can love someone, sacrifice for them, be kind to them, be generous to them out of virtue, out of the desire to follow Christ. You don't necessarily have to feel how someone feels in order to be kind. And actually, I would argue that that kind of love is a lot more powerful." She cites 1 Corinthians 13:6: "Love never rejoices in wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth."

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Define empathy precisely: to feel what another feels (etymology: "in" + "feeling"), as distinct from sympathy (feeling for someone)
- ✅ Distinguish between starting with empathy and ending with empathy; the danger is ending with empathy rather than submitting it to truth
- ✅ Use 1 Corinthians 13:6 as the standard: love "rejoices with the truth," not merely with feelings
- ✅ Apply the "monster in the closet" analogy: empathizing with a child's fear does not require affirming the existence of the monster
- ✅ Ground identity in biology and Genesis 1:27, rejecting dualism (the idea that identity is separate from body)

#### Keep In Mind

- Stuckey does not argue that all empathy is toxic; she says "empathy can be toxic and lead to sin"
- The moniker "toxic empathy" is the title of her book, "Toxic Empathy: How Progressives Exploit Christian Compassion"
- Participant Kyle (a liberal Christian in the audience) presses Stuckey on whether cutting out empathy entirely leads to "toxic individualism and toxic apathy"; she clarifies she does not advocate eliminating empathy
- Participant Shane (using concupiscence language) argues that sin comes from fallen nature, not from empathy itself; Stuckey concedes partial agreement but maintains empathy can initiate a path to affirming sin

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Framing transgender identity affirmation as "toxic empathy" contributes to the documented mental health crisis among transgender Christians
- The argument that gay Christians should remain celibate without robust community support is linked to the elevated suicide risk Gilbert cites (8 to 12 times higher than LGBTQ+ youth from secular or affirming homes, according to research Gilbert references on LGBTQ+ youth in evangelical and conservative Christian settings)
- Using the "monster in the closet" analogy for gender dysphoria trivializes a condition recognized by the American Psychiatric Association

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Equating empathy with sin rather than recognizing it as a neutral capacity that can be directed toward truth or falsehood
  **Solution:** Clarify the distinction: empathy itself is not sin, but empathy untethered from truth can lead to affirming sin
- **Problem:** Conceding that the Samaritan story (Luke 10) is primarily about empathy
  **Solution:** Stuckey argues it is about love and righteous action; the Samaritan's compassion leads to action, not to affirming the injured man's condition
- **Problem:** Claiming God experienced empathy on the cross
  **Solution:** Stuckey notes that God's act of covering Adam and Eve's nakedness (Genesis 3:21) is better understood as provision and atonement, not necessarily empathy as Stuckey defines it

### Topic 3 / View X – Empathy as the Starting Point for Christian Love (Liberal Christians)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Woman Caught in Adultery (John 8:2-11)

Participant Paul (a pastor of a growing church with queer members exhibiting the fruit of the Spirit) cites John 8:2-11: when the woman caught in adultery is brought before Jesus, Jesus "gets down on her level, lowers himself so that he could see the perspective from her point of view." He argues this is empathy in action: Jesus asks questions first and makes comments later, seeking to understand why she sinned rather than immediately calling it sin.

##### Example 2: Romans 12:15 and Weeping with Those Who Weep

Paul and Shane cite Romans 12:15 ("Rejoice with those who rejoice, weep with those who weep") as a biblical command to enter into others' feelings, just as Christ entered into human humanity. Shane argues empathy is a biblical statute that cannot itself be sin; if empathy leads to affirming sin, the sin lies in the fallen human nature that twists it, not in empathy itself.

##### Example 3: The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37)

Participants reference the Good Samaritan as an example of love that enters into another's suffering. The Samaritan, a social outcast, sees the injured man and is moved to action. Liberals argue this demonstrates that empathy—seeing and feeling another's pain—is integral to Christian love, not opposed to it.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Start with empathy as Jesus did: asking questions, lowering oneself to the other's perspective
- ✅ Distinguish empathy (feeling with) from sympathy (feeling for) and compassion (suffering with)
- ✅ Cite Romans 12:15 and Hebrews 4:15 (Christ was tempted in every way as we are) as biblical mandates for entering into others' experience
- ✅ Acknowledge that empathy alone is insufficient; it must lead to action and truth
- ✅ Recognize the Samaritan's empathy as love in action, not mere emotional resonance

#### Keep In Mind

- Liberal Christians do not claim empathy is the endpoint; Paul explicitly states "we start with empathy, but we just don't end with empathy"
- Shane's argument uses the theological category of concupiscence (disordered desire) to argue that sin resides in fallen human nature, not in empathy as a created capacity
- John critiques Stuckey's claim as putting people "on edge" and hardening hearts more than opening them; he worries the message will be misinterpreted as endorsing apathy

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Dismissing empathy as merely a "feeling" can delegitimize the experiences of marginalized Christians, including LGBTQ+ individuals and women facing crisis pregnancies
- The claim that empathy can be "toxic" is experienced by many as a justification for withholding compassion from vulnerable populations

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Claiming that empathy always leads to sin
  **Solution:** Paul and Shane clarify that empathy is a biblical statute; it is human sinfulness that perverts it
- **Problem:** Reducing the Samaritan's action to mere empathy without the costly action
  **Solution:** Note that the Samaritan's empathy led to tangible sacrifice (oil, wine, two denarii, promise of return), not merely emotional validation
- **Problem:** Assuming that feeling another's pain requires agreeing with their conclusions
  **Solution:** Participants on both sides agree: one can empathize with a person's suffering while disagreeing with their moral conclusions

### Topic 4 / View 1 – Progressivism and Christianity Are at Odds (Conservative / Allie Beth Stuckey)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Hebrews 13:8 and Unchanging Truth

Stuckey grounds her definition of progressivism as "the belief that morality and truth evolve with culture and the human experience." She contrasts this with Hebrews 13:8: "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever." She argues that if morality evolves, then Christianity's claim to unchanging truth is undermined. She cites the abolitionist movement: William Wilberforce and others used the same Bible to abolish slavery because they were moving *closer* to what the Bible actually says, not away from it based on cultural trends.

##### Example 2: The Slavery Parallel

Joseph, a participant who grew up in the Church of Christ, invokes Bob Jones University's founder, who used the same conservative biblical framework Stuckey employs to defend racial segregation. He argues that if Stuckey had been alive in the 19th century, her hermeneutic would have placed her on the side of defending slavery because the Bible, by her own admission, does not explicitly condemn race-based chattel slavery. Stuckey counters that Paul's instruction to slaves to obey masters (Ephesians 6:5) describes the existing social order (bond service, or debt servitude for a fixed term to pay off debts) rather than condoning race-based chattel slavery, where people are owned as property for life based on race.

##### Example 3: Women in Church Leadership

Colby challenges Stuckey on 1 Timothy 2:12 ("I do not permit a woman to teach or to assume authority over a man"), noting that Stuckey's own interpretation requires deciding which passages are contextual and which are timeless. Stuckey responds that Paul grounds the prohibition in creation order (1 Timothy 2:13-14: "Adam was formed first, then Eve"), making it normative rather than merely cultural. Colby counters that this is still an interpretation that places Stuckey as the authority, not scripture itself.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Define progressivism clearly before engaging: Stuckey defines it as morality evolving with culture; Colby refines it as "unraveling through discovery"
- ✅ Use Hebrews 13:8 to ground the conservative claim that Christian truth is unchanging
- ✅ Cite Wilberforce and the abolitionists as evidence that Christians can lead moral progress without abandoning biblical authority
- ✅ Acknowledge that progressive Christians do not typically reject biblical authority but reinterpret it through the lens of the Holy Spirit's ongoing guidance
- ✅ Address the slavery parallel directly: distinguish between descriptive biblical regulations for ancient bond service and prescriptive endorsement of modern race-based chattel slavery

#### Keep In Mind

- Stuckey's definition of progressivism is contested by participants; Danny, a pastor, agrees with the definition but says "God is pulling us into greater levels of understanding"
- The conservative framework requires a high view of Pauline authorship and the perspicuity (clear meaning) of scripture
- Liberal participants argue that Jesus himself was "progressive" relative to second-temple Judaism, challenging religious authorities and reinterpreting the law

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Framing progressivism as inherently opposed to Christianity can delegitimize the faith of millions of Christians who hold progressive views on sexuality, gender, and justice
- The slavery parallel is a serious charge; it should be handled with historical precision, distinguishing between ancient bond service and modern chattel slavery
- Claiming that progressive Christians are "unraveling" tradition can feed narratives of Christian persecution

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Conceding that Christianity must evolve to remain relevant
  **Solution:** Distinguish between the church's *understanding* of unchanging truth (which can grow) and the truth itself (which does not change)
- **Problem:** Assuming progressive Christians reject the Bible's authority
  **Solution:** Danny and others explicitly state they take the Bible seriously, reading it in context and wrestling with difficult passages
- **Problem:** Using "progressive" as a pejorative without defining it
  **Solution:** Stuckey's attempt to define it at the start is good practice; the debate shows the term is contested and requires ongoing clarification

### Topic 4 / View X – Progressivism as Christian Growth (Liberal Christians)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Jesus as Progressive Relative to Second-Temple Judaism

Colby argues that in his historical context, Jesus would have been seen as progressive relative to the conservative religion of second-temple Judaism. He notes Jesus' repeated phrase "You have heard it said... but I say to you" as evidence of a progressive posture: challenging received interpretations and leading people into deeper justice. He asks whether Stuckey, if she had been a first-century rabbi challenging Pharisees, would not also be called progressive.

##### Example 2: The Holy Spirit as Guide into Justice

Joseph, a former Church of Christ member, cites Micah 6:8 ("walk humbly, seek justice") and argues that the Holy Spirit in believers' hearts will not disagree with God's word. #### Security & Safety Notes

- Claiming that progressivism inevitably leads to theological liberalism on core doctrines (like the exclusivity of Christ) is not supported by the participants' actual affirmations
- Dismissing the conservative concern about moral relativism can appear to undermine the very foundations of Christian ethics

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Reducing progressive Christianity to moral relativism
  **Solution:** Note that participants affirm objective moral truth grounded in God's character, even as they disagree on specific applications
- **Problem:** Assuming that recognizing one's interpretive fallibility means "making oneself the authority"
  **Solution:** Stuckey makes this charge against Colby, but Colby's position is that all interpreters are finite; humility does not eliminate authority but subjects it to the Spirit and community
- **Problem:** Conflating cultural progressivism with theological progressivism
  **Solution:** Participants distinguish between the political label and the theological posture of growing in Christlikeness

## Steps For Each Topic and Each Topic's View

### Topic 1 / View 1 – Biblical Marriage: One Man, One Woman (Stuckey's Framework)

#### Step 1: Establish the Definition of Marriage from Creation

Present Genesis 1:27 ("male and female he created them") as the foundational creation ordinance. This is not merely a cultural artifact but an ontological statement about human nature as designed by God. Stuckey notes that Jesus cites this passage in Matthew 19:4-5 when the Pharisees test him on divorce, demonstrating that Jesus treated Genesis as normative.

#### Step 2: Show Marriage as an Eternal Reality in Ephesians 5

Cite Ephesians 5:31-32: "'For this reason a man will leave his father and mother and be united to his wife, and the two will become one flesh.' This is a profound mystery—but I am talking about Christ and the church." Stuckey argues that because the Church is the singular bride of Christ, earthly marriage can only reflect this reality when it is monogamous and heterosexual.

#### Step 3: Extend the Argument to Revelation 20

Note that the Bible ends with the marriage supper of the Lamb (Revelation 19-20), where Christ the bridegroom is united to his bride the Church. The canonical arc from Genesis 1 to Revelation 20 frames marriage exclusively as heterosexual and monogamous.

#### Step 4: Address the Polygamy Objection with Prescription vs. Description

When participants cite David, Solomon, or Jacob's multiple wives, distinguish between descriptive narrative (the Bible reports what happened) and prescriptive ideal (what God commands). David's adultery with Bathsheba and Solomon's 700 wives are presented as causes of judgment, not models of holy marriage.

#### Step 5: Address the Genesis Population Problem

When participants ask how Adam and Eve's descendants could populate the earth without incest, Stuckey responds "That's not necessarily true" regarding the necessity of incest, noting that the text does not specify the number of children or the mechanism; the argument is about creation order, not population mechanics.

#### Step 6: Address the Celibacy Question for Gay Christians

Stuckey affirms that all Christians are called to deny fleshly desire (Matthew 16:24) and take up their cross. Marriage is not the ideal of holiness for every Christian (1 Corinthians 7:7-8). She references Rosaria Butterfield, Christopher Yuan, and Becket Cook as examples of Christians who experience same-sex attraction and choose celibacy. She does not mandate marrying a woman; she calls all Christians to deny sin and follow Christ.

### Topic 3 / View 1 – Empathy Can Be Toxic and Lead to Sin (Stuckey's Framework)

#### Step 1: Define Empathy Precisely

Stuckey defines empathy as "to be in someone's feelings" (etymology: "em" + "pathos"), distinguishing it from sympathy (feeling for someone) and compassion (suffering with someone). She argues empathy is a neutral capacity that can lead toward kindness or toward sin depending on whether it is submitted to truth.

#### Step 2: Distinguish Empathy from Love

Stuckey distinguishes empathy from love: "You don't necessarily have to feel how someone feels in order to be kind. And actually, I would argue that that kind of love is a lot more powerful." She cites 1 Corinthians 13:6: "Love never rejoices in wrongdoing, but rejoices with the truth." Love, defined by God, is the endpoint; empathy is not necessarily required.

#### Step 3: Give Specific Examples of Toxic Empathy

Example A: Transgender identity affirmation. Stuckey argues that feeling so deeply for someone who says they are "trapped in the wrong body" that you affirm their gender identity is toxic empathy because it affirms "a lie that you can be born in the wrong body." Example B: Abortion empathy. When we feel deeply for a pregnant woman in crisis and support abortion to alleviate her suffering, we have "sacrificed on the altar of empathy, a living human being."

#### Step 4: Use the Monster in the Closet Analogy

Stuckey compares affirming gender dysphoria to a parent affirming a child's fear of a monster in the closet: "The loving thing to do is not to say, 'Yeah, it is. Sorry.' It's to turn the lights on and say, 'No, that's not a monster in your closet.'" Empathy with the child's fear does not require affirming the false belief.

#### Step 5: Address the Counterargument That Jesus Modeled Empathy

When Paul cites John 8:2-11 (the woman caught in adultery), Stuckey acknowledges that Jesus showed compassion but argues his endpoint was truth: "He who is without sin, cast the first stone" leads to her being told to "go and sin no more." The endpoint is repentance, not mere affirmation.

### Topic 4 / View 1 – Progressivism and Christianity Are at Odds (Stuckey's Framework)
