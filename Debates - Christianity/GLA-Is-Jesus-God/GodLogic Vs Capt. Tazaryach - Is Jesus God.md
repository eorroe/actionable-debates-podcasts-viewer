# GodLogic Vs Capt. Tazaryach | Is Jesus God?

## Overview

A public debate between Avery from GodLogic Apologetics and Captain Cesar (JP) of Capt. Tazaryach on the question of whether Jesus Christ is God. Avery defends the position that Jesus is the Divine Son of God, pre-existent and eternal with the Father and the Spirit. Captain Cesar argues that Jesus is not God, maintaining that Jesus is a created being subordinate to the Most High Father. The debate centers on the interpretation of key biblical passages and the meaning of titles such as "firstborn," "Word of God," "Lord of lords," and "Son of God."

## When to Follow Debate

- When examining whether biblical titles and passages support the deity of Christ
- When comparing Trinitarian and Unitarian interpretations of Scripture
- When studying the meaning of "firstborn of all creation" in Colossians 1
- When analyzing the relationship between the Father and the Son in the New Testament
- When discussing whether Jesus has a God or is subordinate to the Father
- When analyzing the meaning and implications of Jesus' statements about his own identity and authority

## Glossary / Prerequisite Terms To Know Before Following Debate

- **Firstborn of all creation**: A title applied to Christ in Colossians 1:15; debated as either indicating preeminence among creation or literal first created being.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: Refers to preeminence and priority over all creation, not a created status; Christ is the heir of all things.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: Means literally the first thing created by the Father, after which all else was made through him.
- **Word of God (Logos)**: The title given to Christ in John 1:1; debated as either identifying Christ as God or as a separate created messenger/authority.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: The Word is eternally God, the second person of the Trinity, through whom all things were made.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: The Word is a created being, the first creation of God, who acted as God's spokesman and agent.
- **Elohim**: A Hebrew word often translated "God"; debated as either indicating the plural nature of the Godhead or simply meaning power, authority, or ruler.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: Supports the plurality within the Godhead, consistent with Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: A plural form used for majesty or authority, not evidence of multiple persons in the Godhead; can refer to angels, princes, or judges.
- **Lord of lords**: A title applied to both the Father (Deuteronomy 10:17) and Christ (Revelation 19:16); debated as either shared or exclusive.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: Both Father and Son hold the title, affirming the deity of Christ.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: The Father alone is the true Lord of lords; Christ receives the title as a delegated ruler under the Father.
- **Subordination**: The concept that the Son is under the authority of the Father; debated as either a role in the Godhead or evidence that the Son is not God.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: Voluntary subordination in role and economy, not a lesser nature; the Son shares the same divine nature as the Father.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: Proof that Jesus is a separate, lesser being who obeys and reports to a higher God.
- **Trinity**: The doctrine that one God exists eternally as three distinct persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: The orthodox Christian understanding of the Godhead, supported by the full counsel of Scripture.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: A later doctrinal invention not found in the Bible; contradicts the clear teaching of one God, the Father.
- **Son of God**: A title for Jesus; debated as either indicating literal divine sonship and equality with God or a metaphorical relationship of submission.
  - From Trinitarian Viewpoint: Denotes the unique, eternal relationship between the second person of the Trinity and the Father.
  - From Unitarian Viewpoint: A metaphorical title for a created being whom God appointed as his representative and obedient servant.

## Discussed Topics

### Topic 1: The Meaning of "Firstborn of All Creation" (Colossians 1:15)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: David as "Firstborn" (Psalm 89:27)

Example text: Captain Cesar argued that "firstborn" does not always mean literally first in birth order, citing David who was called the firstborn of all kings though he was not the first king of Israel nor the oldest in his family. Avery countered that David's "firstborn" status referred to preeminence, not creation.

##### Example 2: Israel as God's "Firstborn" (Exodus 4:22)

Example text: Captain Cesar noted that Exodus 4:22 calls Israel God's "firstborn," which is clearly metaphorical since Israel is a nation, not a literal child. He used this to argue that "firstborn" can be a metaphor for a special relationship. Avery agreed that father-son language can be metaphorical but maintained that in Colossians 1, the context establishes Christ's preeminence over all creation, not a created origin.

##### Example 3: The Word as Creator (John 1:3)

Example text: Avery quoted John 1:3: "All things were made through him, and without him was not anything made that was made." He argued that if Christ created all things, he cannot himself be part of creation. Captain Cesar responded that Colossians 1:16-17 shows Christ created all things "through him" by authority delegated from the Father, not by his own independent power.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Examine the immediate context of a passage before interpreting key terms
- ✅ Compare Scripture with Scripture to build a coherent theology
- ✅ Distinguish between literal and figurative language based on context
- ✅ Consider the original Greek or Hebrew meaning of key terms
- ❌ Do not assume a term has only one meaning across all contexts
- ❌ Do not isolate a verse from its surrounding passage
- ❌ Do not use a metaphor to override explicit statements of fact
- ❌ Avoid charging the other side with positions they did not state

#### Keep In Mind

- The debate hinges on whether "firstborn" in Colossians 1:15 refers to preeminence or literal priority in creation.
- Captain Cesar holds that Colossians 1:19 ("it pleased the Father that in him should all fullness dwell") shows the Father gave Christ his authority and fullness, implying Christ was not eternally equal with the Father.
- Avery maintains that "firstborn" in Colossians 1 is a title of preeminence, not a statement about origin, supported by the parallel description of Christ as "the image of the invisible God."
- Both sides agree that the Father is the ultimate source of all things, but disagree on whether Christ is a created agent or the eternal Creator.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Misusing "firstborn" to mean only literal first created being without considering its use for preeminence.
  **Solution:** Study the full range of biblical usage for "firstborn" and evaluate the context of Colossians 1 holistically.
- **Problem:** Conflating subordination in role with subordination in nature.
  **Solution:** Distinguish between functional authority relationships within the Godhead and the essential nature of the persons.
- **Problem:** Arguing in circles: "Christ is created because he is firstborn" and "he is firstborn because he is created."
  **Solution:** Establish the meaning of terms from the text itself before using them to prove a position.

### Topic 2: The Meaning of John 1:1 - "The Word Was God"

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The Word as Power, Not Person

Example text: Captain Cesar argued that "God" in John 1:1 comes from the Hebrew concept of Elohim, which he defined as power, authority, messenger, or ruler—not necessarily the Most High God. He stated, "That word there for God is power. It's not making him God. It's just that simple." Avery responded that John 1:1 distinguishes the Word from God the Father ("the Word was with God") while affirming the Word's deity ("and the Word was God").

##### Example 2: Elohim Used for Angels and Rulers

Example text: Captain Cesar cited Psalms 82:6 ("I said, Ye are gods") and argued that Elohim is used for princes, angels, messengers, and judges, so when John 1:1 calls the Word "God," it does not mean the Most High. Avery countered that John 1:1 uses theos (Greek for God) and that the context of John 1:3 ("through him all things were made") proves the Word cannot be a created being or merely a powerful ruler.

##### Example 3: The Word Becoming Flesh (John 1:14)

Example text: Avery pointed out that John 1:14 says "the Word became flesh," and since flesh has a God (John 20:17), Jesus speaking of his God refers to the Father in his human nature. Captain Cesar argued this shows the Word (a created being) was given a body and thus had a God over him.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Analyze the Greek text and its nuance when discussing John 1:1
- ✅ Consider the prologue of John as a whole (John 1:1-18) rather than isolated verses
- ✅ Distinguish between the Word's pre-incarnate state and his incarnate state
- ❌ Do not redefine "God" to mean only "power" when the text clearly identifies the Word as the Creator
- ❌ Do not use the plural form of Elohim to prove polytheism or multiple gods in the Godhead without context
- ❌ Do not ignore the theological context of the Gospel of John, which presents Jesus as the divine Son

#### Keep In Mind

- Avery's position: John 1:1 affirms the deity of Christ without confusing him with the Father; the Word is distinct from God the Father ("with God") but is Himself God.
- Captain Cesar's position: John 1:1 uses "God" in a functional sense (Elohim = power/authority), not identifying the Word as the Most High; the Word was the first creation of God.
- The debate over John 1:1 is foundational: if the Word is God, then Jesus is God; if the Word is a created messenger, then Jesus is not God.
- Both sides reference the same Greek concept of Elohim but draw opposite conclusions from it.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Reducing "theos" in John 1:1 to mere "power" without explaining why the Word is distinguished from "theon" (God) in the same verse.
  **Solution:** Address the qualitative vs. definite force of the anarthrous "theos" in John 1:1c and how it functions in Greek grammar.
- **Problem:** Assuming that because angels or rulers are called "gods" in Scripture, Jesus must be the same category.
  **Solution:** Compare the nature, works, and worship of Christ with created beings; only the Creator is worthy of worship.
- **Problem:** Ignoring John 1:3 and 1:10 when interpreting John 1:1.
  **Solution:** Let the wider prologue interpret the key verse; John 1:3 proves the Word is uncreated.

### Topic 3: Whether Jesus Has a God / Is Subordinate to the Father

#### Examples

##### Example 1: John 20:17 - "I Go to My God and Your God"

Example text: Captain Cesar quoted John 20:17 where Jesus says, "I go to my Father and your Father; and to my God and your God." He argued this proves Jesus has a God over him and therefore cannot be God. Avery responded that this refers to Jesus' human nature, as the incarnate Christ had a God just as his followers do, while in his divine nature he is eternally equal with the Father.

##### Example 2: 1 Corinthians 8:5-6 - "One God, the Father" and "One Lord, Jesus Christ"

Example text: Captain Cesar argued that 1 Corinthians 8:6 clearly identifies only the Father as God and Christ as Lord (a ruler, not God). Avery countered that "Lord" (Kurios) is also used for Yahweh in the Septuagint, and that the verse presents the Father as the source of all things and Christ as the agent through whom all things exist, consistent with Trinitarian economics.

##### Example 3: 1 Corinthians 11:3 - "The Head of Christ Is God"

Example text: Captain Cesar cited 1 Corinthians 11:3 to prove a hierarchy: God over Christ, Christ over man, man over woman. Avery argued this verse speaks to functional headship and role, not nature; just as a captain is head of a soldier without being a different species, the Father is head of Christ without Christ being a different nature from the Father.

##### Example 4: Matthew 28:18 - "All Power Is Given to Me"

Example text: Captain Cesar pointed to Matthew 28:18 where Jesus says all power is given to him, arguing this proves he did not possess it inherently. Avery agreed that in his incarnate state Jesus humbled himself and received authority, but maintained this does not negate his eternal divine nature, referencing Philippians 2:6-7.

##### Example 5: John 17:5 - Jesus Asking for Glory Back

Example text: Captain Cesar noted that in John 17:5, Jesus prays, "And now, O Father, glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was," arguing this shows Jesus once had glory but lost it and needed it restored. Avery explained that Jesus willingly laid aside the independent exercise of his divine attributes in the incarnation but never ceased to be God.

##### Example 6: Gethsemane - "Not My Will, but Yours"

Example text: Captain Cesar cited Luke 22:42 where Jesus prays, "Not my will, but thine, be done," arguing this shows two separate wills and thus two separate beings. Avery explained that Jesus' human will submitted to his divine will; in his humanity he experienced genuine struggle, but in his divinity he was perfectly aligned with the Father.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between Christ's pre-incarnate, incarnate, and exalted states when discussing his relationship to the Father
- ✅ Recognize that functional subordination does not necessarily imply ontological subordination
- ✅ Use the full counsel of Scripture rather than proof-texting isolated verses
- ❌ Do not use Jesus' human statements to deny his divine nature
- ❌ Do not confuse the economic Trinity (roles in history) with the ontological Trinity (essential being)
- ❌ Do not ignore passages that affirm Christ's deity when building a case against it

#### Keep In Mind

- Avery's position: Jesus' statements about having a God refer to his human nature; in his divine nature he is eternally God, equal with the Father. Subordination is voluntary and functional, not essential.
- Captain Cesar's position: Jesus consistently presents himself as subordinate to the Father, having a God over him, receiving authority and glory from the Father, and obeying the Father's will. This proves he is not the Most High God.
- Key texts for Avery: Philippians 2:6-7 (emptied himself), Colossians 2:9 (fullness of deity dwells bodily), Hebrews 1:3 (upholds all things by the word of his power).
- Key texts for Captain Cesar: John 20:17, 1 Corinthians 8:6, 1 Corinthians 11:3, John 17:5, Matthew 28:18.
- The debate turns on whether the Incarnation introduced a real subordination that reflects Christ's essential nature or only a temporary role for redemptive purposes.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Using John 20:17 to prove Jesus is not God without considering the incarnation and the two natures of Christ.
  **Solution:** Acknowledge the mystery of the incarnation and explain that the divine and human natures are distinct yet united in one person.
- **Problem:** Charging the Trinity with contradiction because of the plurality of persons while affirming monotheism.
  **Solution:** Clarify that Trinitarian monotheism is monotheism in being (one essence) and polytheism in persons (three persons), not Tritheism.
- **Problem:** Equating "given authority" with "not inherently possessing authority."
  **Solution:** Distinguish between the eternal possession of divine attributes and the incarnate exercise of those attributes under the Father's will.

### Topic 4: The Title "Lord of Lords" and the Throne in Heaven

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Deuteronomy 10:17 - Yahweh as Lord of Lords

Example text: Captain Cesar quoted Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the Lord your God is God of gods, and Lord of lords." He argued this proves the Father alone is the supreme Lord of lords and that no one can be higher. Avery responded that Revelation 19:16 identifies Christ as "King of kings, and Lord of lords" in the context of his final victory, and that this title belongs to Christ in his exaltation.

##### Example 2: Revelation 19:16 - Christ as King of Kings and Lord of Lords

Example text: Avery read Revelation 19:16: "And he hath on his vesture and on his thigh a name written, King of kings, and Lord of lords." He argued this clearly identifies the one who comes with the wrath of Almighty God as King of kings and Lord of lords. Captain Cesar countered that the verse says he has the name written on his garment, not that it is his personal name, and that the Father is still the supreme Lord.

##### Example 3: Two Thrones in Heaven

Example text: Captain Cesar acknowledged two thrones—one for the Father and one for Christ—but argued this proves two separate beings, not one God in three persons. Avery cited Revelation 3:21 where Christ sits with the Father on his throne, arguing this shows shared authority and glory, not separate gods.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Read apocalyptic literature (Revelation) with attention to its symbolic and literal elements
- ✅ Let clear passages interpret symbolic ones
- ✅ Consider the full vision in Revelation 19 in its context
- ❌ Do not read titles in isolation from the one who bears them
- ❌ Do not assume that two thrones necessarily means two gods
- ❌ Do not ignore the Trinitarian context of the Father-Son relationship when discussing heavenly thrones

#### Keep In Mind

- Avery's position: Christ is called King of kings and Lord of lords in Revelation 19, sharing in the supreme authority of the Father; two thrones do not divide the Godhead but reflect the functional distinction within the Trinity.
- Captain Cesar's position: The Father alone is the Lord of lords (Deuteronomy 10:17); Christ may bear the title as a delegated ruler but is not the Most High God.
- Captain Cesar emphasized that "King of kings" and "Lord of lords" written on Christ's garment does not make it his personal name or prove his deity.
- Avery emphasized that Revelation 19:16 shows Christ coming with the wrath of Almighty God, exercising supreme authority.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Treating apocalyptic titles as merely honorary without examining who bears them and in what context.
  **Solution:** Read Revelation 19:11-16 as a whole and compare the titles given to Christ with those given to the Father.
- **Problem:** Assuming that because the Father is Lord of lords, Christ cannot also be Lord of lords.
  **Solution:** Recognize that the same title can be shared within the Godhead without contradiction, as both Father and Son are fully divine.
- **Problem:** Using two thrones to prove two gods without addressing the unity of essence and will between Father and Son.
  **Solution:** Explain the distinction between persons and essence in Trinitarian theology.

### Topic 5: The Nature of the Father-Son Relationship

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Israel as God's Son (Exodus 4:22)

Example text: Captain Cesar cited Exodus 4:22: "Israel is my son, even my firstborn," to prove that "son of God" is a metaphor for a chosen people or servant, not literal divine sonship. Avery agreed that some uses of "son" are metaphorical but argued that Jesus' sonship is unique and literal, not merely a figure of speech.

##### Example 2: Abraham as Father of Faith (Romans 4:11-12)

Example text: Captain Cesar argued that just as Abraham is called the father of faith without faith being his literal child, God the Father is called Father without Christ being his literal son. Avery distinguished between metaphorical fatherhood (Abraham as father of faith) and the unique eternal relationship between the Father and the Son.

##### Example 3: Isaiah 53:10 - "He shall see his seed"

Example text: Captain Cesar noted that Isaiah 53:10 speaks of Christ seeing his "seed," which he argued is metaphorical for those who believe through Christ. Avery agreed that "seed" here is metaphorical for spiritual offspring but maintained this does not negate the literal Father-Son relationship within the Godhead.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between metaphorical and literal uses of familial language in Scripture
- ✅ Recognize that biblical metaphor does not necessarily negate underlying reality
- ✅ Consider the uniqueness of Jesus' sonship compared to all other uses of "son" in the Bible
- ❌ Do not reduce all father-son language to metaphor without examining the specific context
- ❌ Do not assume that because Israel or Abraham is called "son" or "father," Jesus' sonship must be the same category
- ❌ Do not ignore the claims Jesus made about his unique relationship with the Father

#### Keep In Mind

- Avery's position: Jesus' sonship is unique, eternal, and literal; he is the only begotten Son of God, not merely a metaphorical son like Israel or a spiritual descendant like believers.
- Captain Cesar's position: "Son of God" is a title of submission and office, not literal divine sonship; Jesus was a righteous man whom God adopted or appointed as his son, not an eternal being equal with the Father.
- Captain Cesar emphasized that if the Father has always been the Father, and fathers exist before sons, then the Son must have had a beginning.
- Avery countered that the Father-Son relationship is an eternal, internal relationship within the Godhead, not a temporal, biological one.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming all father-son language operates on the same plane, whether human, national, or divine.
  **Solution:** Categorize the different uses of familial language in Scripture and evaluate Jesus' sonship in its unique context.
- **Problem:** Arguing that because the Father is eternal, the Son must be created.
  **Solution:** Explain the eternal generation of the Son in Trinitarian theology: the Son is eternally begotten, not created in time.
- **Problem:** Reducing Jesus to a mere prophet or messenger because he is called "Son."
  **Solution:** Examine the specific claims Jesus made about himself and the worship he received.

### Topic 6: Jesus' Claims to Identity and Worship

#### Examples

##### Example 1: John 10:30-33 - "I and My Father Are One"

Example text: Captain Cesar noted that in John 10:30-33, the Jews tried to stone Jesus for blasphemy because he claimed to be God. Jesus responded by quoting Psalm 82:6 ("I said, Ye are gods") rather than affirming the charge. Avery argued that Jesus did not refute the claim because he was indeed claiming equality with God; he directed them to Psalm 82 to show that if human judges could be called "gods," then the Son of God could certainly claim to be one with the Father.

##### Example 2: Matthew 19:16-17 - "Why Callest Thou Me Good?"

Example text: Captain Cesar cited Matthew 19:17 where Jesus says, "Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is God." He argued this proves Jesus did not consider himself God or even good in himself. Avery responded that Jesus was directing the young man to the only source of true goodness—God—and that by so doing, Jesus was implicitly identifying himself with that goodness.

##### Example 3: Thomas's Confession (John 20:28)

Example text: Avery noted that Thomas called Jesus "My Lord and my God" in John 20:28, and Jesus did not rebuke him. Captain Cesar argued that "God" here is used in the sense of Elohim (ruler/authority) rather than the Most High, and that Jesus accepted the title in the same way he accepted "good" without being God.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Let the immediate narrative context shape the interpretation of Jesus' words
- ✅ Pay attention to what Jesus' opponents understood him to be claiming
- ✅ Note whether Jesus accepted or rejected titles and worship
- ❌ Do not force Jesus' words into a Unitarian framework by redefining "God" as "ruler" when the context clearly points to deity
- ❌ Do not ignore Thomas's direct address to the resurrected Christ as "My God"
- ❌ Do not assume Jesus' question in Matthew 19:17 is a denial of his own goodness

#### Keep In Mind

- Avery's position: Jesus' statements consistently point to his deity; his refusal to rebuke Thomas's "My Lord and my God" is a tacit affirmation. The Jews understood him to be claiming equality with God, and his response in John 10 confirms he is the Son of God, one with the Father.
- Captain Cesar's position: Jesus never claimed to be God; he consistently presented himself as the Son of God, a subordinate agent of the Father. When accused of making himself God, he redirected the conversation rather than affirming the charge.
- The debate over John 10:33-36 is central: did Jesus affirm or deny the charge of blasphemy?
- Both sides agree that Psalm 82:6 is significant, but disagree on how Jesus applied it.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- None.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Reading modern theological categories back into the text without letting the text speak for itself.
  **Solution:** Approach each passage with historical-grammatical exegesis, considering the original audience and authorial intent.
- **Problem:** Ignoring the response of Jesus' contemporaries to his claims.
  **Solution:** Pay attention to how the Pharisees, disciples, and other characters in the narrative understood Jesus' words.
- **Problem:** Using Jesus' rhetorical question in Matthew 19:17 to prove he is not good/God.
  **Solution:** Recognize that Jesus was challenging the young man's understanding, not denying his own deity or goodness.

## Steps For Each Topic and Each Topic's View

### Topic 1 / View 1 (Avery - "Firstborn" Means Preeminence, Not Created Status)

#### Step 1: Establish the Context of Colossians 1:15-20

Read Colossians 1:15-20 as a continuous passage. Note that verse 15 calls Christ "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of every creature," but verse 16 immediately follows with "for by him were all things created." The "for" (gar in Greek) explains why he is firstborn: not because he was created, but because he is the heir and preeminent one over all creation.

#### Step 2: Use David as an Analogy for "Firstborn"

Reference Psalm 89:27 where David is called "my firstborn, higher than the kings of the earth." David was neither the first king of Israel nor the oldest in his family. "Firstborn" here signifies preeminence, rank, and inheritance, not chronological priority. Apply this to Christ: he is firstborn in rank and inheritance, not in time.

#### Step 3: Distinguish Between Metaphorical and Literal Uses of "Son"

Acknowledge that "son" is used metaphorically in Scripture (Israel as God's son in Exodus 4:22, believers as sons of God in Romans 8:14). However, Jesus' sonship is unique: he is the "only begotten Son" (John 3:16), not a metaphorical son. The Father-Son relationship within the Godhead is an eternal, personal relationship, not a metaphor for a covenant or office.

#### Step 4: Address Colossians 1:19

When Captain Cesar cites Colossians 1:19 ("It pleased the Father that in him should all fullness dwell"), explain that this refers to the incarnation and the indwelling of the fullness of deity in Christ bodily (Colossians 2:9). It does not mean the Father created Christ and then gave him fullness; rather, the full divine nature dwelled in the incarnate Christ.

### Topic 1 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - "Firstborn" Means First Created Being)

#### Step 1: Establish the Literal Meaning of "Firstborn"

Argue that "firstborn" (prototokos) in Colossians 1:15 should be understood in its plain, literal sense: the first one born or created. Compare with other uses of "firstborn" in the Bible where it clearly refers to chronological priority (e.g., Exodus 13:2, Numbers 3:12). If the author meant preeminence, he would have used a different term.

#### Step 2: Emphasize Colossians 1:19 as Proof of Creation

Cite Colossians 1:19: "For it pleased the Father that in him should all fullness dwell." Argue that if Christ were eternally God, he would not need to have fullness "dwell" in him; fullness implies something given, added, or granted by the Father after creation. The Father created Christ and then chose to dwell his fullness in him.

#### Step 3: Use the Father-Son Metaphor to Prove Chronological Priority

Argue that in every literal father-son relationship, the father exists before the son. If God has always been the Father, and if "Father" is not merely a metaphor but describes a real relationship, then the Son must have had a beginning. The Father created the Son and then gave him authority to create all else (John 1:3, Colossians 1:16).

#### Step 4: Distinguish Between the Word and the Son

Argue that "the Word" in John 1:1 is not the pre-existent Christ as a divine person, but God's spokesman or messenger—the first and greatest creation—through whom God spoke and acted. The Word became flesh (John 1:14) when God gave this created being a body, not when an eternal God took on humanity.

### Topic 2 / View 1 (Avery - "The Word Was God" Affirms Christ's Deity)

#### Step 1: Read John 1:1 in Its Grammatical Context

Present John 1:1: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." Note the two distinct clauses: "the Word was with God" (distinction) and "the Word was God" (deity). The Word is distinct from the Father ("with God") but shares the same divine nature ("was God").

#### Step 2: Emphasize John 1:3 as Proof of Uncreated Status

Quote John 1:3: "All things were made by him; and without him was not any thing made that was made." If the Word created all things, he cannot himself be part of creation. A created being cannot create himself. Therefore, the Word must be eternally uncreated, sharing in the divine nature.

#### Step 3: Address the Elohim Argument

Acknowledge that Elohim is a plural form but note that it is consistently used for the one true God in the Old Testament (Deuteronomy 6:4). While Elohim can sometimes refer to judges or rulers (Psalm 82:6), John 1:1 uses the Greek theos, which unequivocally means God. The prologue of John presents the Word as the Creator, not a creature.

#### Step 4: Connect the Word to Christ in John 1:14

Quote John 1:14: "And the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us." Identify the Word with Jesus Christ. Since the Word is God and the Word became flesh, Jesus is God in the flesh. This is the consistent testimony of the Gospel of John.

### Topic 2 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - "The Word Was God" Means Powerful Ruler, Not the Most High)

#### Step 1: Define Elohim as Power, Authority, or Ruler

Argue that the Hebrew word Elohim, and its Greek equivalent theos in John 1:1, does not exclusively mean the Most High God. It can refer to anyone with power, authority, or rulership—angels, princes, judges, or appointed messengers. When John 1:1 says "the Word was God," it means the Word was a powerful divine being, not the Most High God himself.

#### Step 2: Use Psalm 82:6 to Show "Gods" Are Created Beings

Reference Psalm 82:6 where God says, "I said, Ye are gods; and all of you are children of the most High." Argue that these "gods" are human judges or rulers, not eternal beings. If mere humans can be called "gods" in Scripture, then the Word being called "God" in John 1:1 does not make him the Most High. It simply means he is a mighty ruler or messenger.

#### Step 3: Argue the Word Was Created Before All Else

Maintain that John 1:1 says "In the beginning was the Word," meaning the Word had a beginning. Only the Father has no beginning. The Word was the first thing the Father created, and through the Word, the Father made everything else. The Word is the beginning of creation, not eternal.

#### Step 4: Explain "The Word Was God" as Functional Deity

Argue that "the Word was God" means the Word acted as God's representative, carrying God's authority and power. This is similar to how Moses was made "a god to Pharaoh" (Exodus 7:1). The Word was not inherently God but was given divine authority and power to act on God's behalf.

### Topic 3 / View 1 (Avery - Subordination Is Functional, Not Ontological)

#### Step 1: Distinguish Between Nature and Role

Explain that Jesus' subordination to the Father is a matter of role and function in the economy of salvation, not a difference in essential nature. A human captain is the head of his soldiers, but this does not make the soldiers less human. Likewise, the Father is the head of Christ (1 Corinthians 11:3), but this does not mean Christ is less divine.

#### Step 2: Explain the Incarnation and Two Natures

Argue that when Jesus said "I go to my God and your God" (John 20:17), he spoke as a man, in his human nature. The incarnate Christ had a God because he took on a true human nature. In his divine nature, he is eternally God, equal with the Father. The two natures are distinct yet united in the one person of Christ.

#### Step 3: Address Matthew 28:18 and John 17:5

Explain that Matthew 28:18 ("All power is given unto me in heaven and in earth") refers to the authority Christ received in his incarnate state after his resurrection, not the loss of divine attributes. John 17:5 ("Glorify thou me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was") shows that Christ once possessed glory with the Father before the world began and asked to have it restored in his exaltation.

#### Step 4: Use Philippians 2:6-7 to Explain Voluntary Humiliation

Quote Philippians 2:6-7: "Who, being in the form of God, thought it not robbery to be equal with God: but made himself of no reputation, and took upon him the form of a servant." Christ voluntarily laid aside the independent exercise of his divine prerogatives in the incarnation, but he never ceased to be God. His prayers in Gethsemane reflect the struggle of his human will, not a lack of divine unity.

### Topic 3 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - Subordination Proves Jesus Is Not God)

#### Step 1: Emphasize That Jesus Consistently Presented Himself as Subordinate

Argue that Jesus never claimed to be God but consistently presented himself as the Son of God, under the authority of the Father. In John 20:17, he explicitly calls the Father "my God." In Matthew 28:18, he says all power was "given" to him. In John 17, he asks the Father to give him back the glory he once had. These statements prove someone is over Jesus.

#### Step 2: Use 1 Corinthians 8:5-6 to Establish Monotheism and Subordination

Quote 1 Corinthians 8:5-6: "For though there be that are called gods, whether in heaven or in earth, as there be gods many, and lords many, but to us there is but one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we in him; and one Lord Jesus Christ, by whom are all things." Argue that Paul clearly identifies only the Father as God and Christ as Lord (a subordinate title, not deity).

#### Step 3: Cite 1 Corinthians 11:3 for Hierarchy

Quote 1 Corinthians 11:3: "But I would have you know, that the head of every man is Christ; and the head of the woman is the man; and the head of Christ is God." Argue that this establishes a clear hierarchy: God over Christ, Christ over man, man over woman. If the head of Christ is God, then Christ is not God.

#### Step 4: Argue That Jesus Had a Separate Will from the Father

Cite Luke 22:42: "Not my will, but thine, be done." Argue that if Jesus and the Father were the same God, they would have the same will. The fact that Jesus prayed for the Father's will to be done over his own proves two separate beings, not one God in two persons.

### Topic 4 / View 1 (Avery - Christ Shares the Title "Lord of Lords" as Deity)

#### Step 1: Present Revelation 19:16 in Context

Quote Revelation 19:16: "And he hath on his vesture and on his thigh a name written, King of kings, and Lord of lords." Explain that this vision shows the exalted Christ coming in glory and victory. The title "Lord of lords" is not merely a delegated title but an affirmation of his supreme authority as King of kings.

#### Step 2: Connect Deuteronomy 10:17 and Revelation 19:16

Acknowledge that Deuteronomy 10:17 calls Yahweh "God of gods, and Lord of lords." Argue that if the Father is Lord of lords, and Christ is also called Lord of lords in Revelation 19, this supports the deity of Christ. Both Father and Son share in the divine nature and authority.

#### Step 3: Use Revelation 3:21 for Shared Throne

Quote Revelation 3:21: "To him that overcometh will I grant to sit with me in my throne, even as I also overcame, and am set down with my Father in his throne." Argue that Christ sits with the Father on his throne, sharing in his rule. This is consistent with the unity of the Godhead, not evidence of two separate gods.

#### Step 4: Address the "Name on the Garment" Point

When Captain Cesar argues that "King of kings, and Lord of lords" is written on Christ's garment rather than being his personal name, explain that in apocalyptic literature, names written on garments signify identity and character. The fact that this title is written on his robe and thigh signifies that Christ himself is the King of kings and Lord of lords.

### Topic 4 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - The Father Alone Is the Supreme Lord of Lords)

#### Step 1: Affirm Deuteronomy 10:17 as Exclusive to the Father

Quote Deuteronomy 10:17: "For the Lord your God is God of gods, and Lord of lords, a great God, a mighty, and a terrible." Argue that this title belongs exclusively to the Father, the Most High. No one can be higher than the Lord of lords. If Christ were also Lord of lords, there would be two supreme beings, which contradicts monotheism.

#### Step 2: Distinguish Between a Title Written on Garment and Personal Identity

Argue that Revelation 19:16 says the name "King of kings, and Lord of lords" is written on Christ's vesture and thigh, not that it is his personal name or identity. A soldier may have a title on his uniform without being the supreme commander. Christ bears the title as a delegated ruler under the Father.

#### Step 3: Maintain That Two Thrones Prove Two Beings

Argue that if there are two thrones—one for the Father and one for Christ—this proves two separate beings, not one God. The Father sits on his throne, and Christ sits on a separate throne. Even if they rule together, they are distinct entities, not one divine essence in three persons.

#### Step 4: Explain 1 Corinthians 8:5-6 as Excluding Christ from Being God

Argue that 1 Corinthians 8:6 is explicit: "To us there is but one God, the Father." Christ is called "Lord," but in the context of 1 Corinthians 8, "Lord" means master or ruler, not the Most High God. The chapter's whole point is that there is only one God, the Father, and many so-called lords.

### Topic 5 / View 1 (Avery - Jesus' Sonship Is Unique, Eternal, and Literal)

#### Step 1: Distinguish Jesus' Sonship from Metaphorical Uses

Argue that while "son" is used metaphorically in Scripture (Israel as God's son in Exodus 4:22, believers as sons of God in Romans 8:14), Jesus' sonship is unique. He is the "only begotten Son" (John 3:16), not a metaphorical son. The Father-Son relationship within the Trinity is an eternal, personal relationship, not a figure of speech.

#### Step 2: Use John 3:16 to Establish Literal Sonship

Quote John 3:16: "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only begotten Son." Argue that if Jesus were merely a created being or a metaphorical son, the verse would lose its force. The unique, eternal sonship of Christ is what makes the Gospel good news: God gave his own equal, not a created servant.

#### Step 3: Address the "Father Always Being Father" Argument

When Captain Cesar argues that if the Father has always been the Father, he must have always had a Son, explain that this reflects an eternal relationship within the Godhead. The Father is eternally Father, the Son is eternally Son, and the Spirit is eternally Spirit. This is not a temporal sequence but an eternal distinction of persons within the one divine essence.

#### Step 4: Use the Transfiguration as Proof of Unique Sonship

Reference Matthew 17:5 where the Father speaks from heaven: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased; hear ye him." Argue that the Father's voice from heaven confirms the unique, literal sonship of Christ and commands the disciples to hear him as the divine Son.

### Topic 5 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - "Son of God" Is a Title of Office, Not Literal Divine Sonship)

#### Step 1: Use Israel as a Type of God's Son

Argue that Exodus 4:22 calls Israel "my son, even my firstborn," yet Israel is a nation, not a literal son of God. This proves that "son of God" can be a metaphorical title for a people or a servant whom God has chosen and appointed. Jesus, as the ultimate servant and representative of God, is called "Son of God" in the same sense.

#### Step 2: Use Abraham as Father of Faith as a Metaphor

Argue that Abraham is called the "father of faith" (Romans 4:11-12), yet faith is not his literal child. This shows that fatherhood language in Scripture can be metaphorical. Just as Abraham is the father of all who believe without being their biological father, God is the Father of Christ in the sense of appointing and authorizing him, not in the sense of literal divine generation.

#### Step 3: Argue That "Only Begotten" Means Chosen, Not Eternal

Argue that "only begotten" (monogenēs) in John 3:16 means unique or one of a kind, not eternally generated. Isaac was Abraham's "only begotten" son (Hebrews 11:17), yet Isaac was not eternally begotten; he was born in time. Likewise, Jesus is God's unique, one-of-a-kind Son in his role and office, not an eternal being equal with the Father.

#### Step 4: Maintain That Jesus Was a Man Chosen by God

Argue that Jesus was a righteous man whom God appointed, anointed, and sent as his Son. He was not an eternal being who took on humanity but a human being whom God exalted to a position of authority. His sonship is therefore a title of office and relationship, not a statement about his essential nature.

### Topic 6 / View 1 (Avery - Jesus Claimed and Accepted Divine Identity)

#### Step 1: Examine John 10:30-33 and Jesus' Response

Quote John 10:30: "I and my Father are one." Note that the Jews understood this as a claim to deity and tried to stone him for blasphemy (John 10:33). Jesus did not say, "I did not claim to be God." Instead, he quoted Psalm 82:6 to show that if human judges could be called "gods," then the Son of God, whom the Father set apart and sent into the world, could certainly claim to be one with the Father. His response confirms, not denies, his claim.

#### Step 2: Note Thomas's Confession in John 20:28

Quote John 20:28: "And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God." Note that Jesus did not rebuke Thomas for calling him God. If Jesus were not God, this would have been the perfect opportunity to correct a false confession. Instead, Jesus accepted the worship and the title.

#### Step 3: Analyze Matthew 19:16-17 as a Challenge, Not a Denial

Argue that when Jesus said, "Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is God," he was not denying his own goodness or deity. Rather, he was challenging the young man's superficial use of "good" and directing him to the only source of true goodness—God. Jesus is that goodness incarnate.

#### Step 4: Use Jesus' Works and Worship as Evidence

Argue that Jesus' works (forgiving sins, controlling nature, raising the dead) and his acceptance of worship (Matthew 28:17, Luke 24:52) are consistent with divine identity. Only God can forgive sins (Mark 2:7), and only God deserves worship. Jesus' actions confirm what his words claim.

### Topic 6 / View 2 (Capt. Tazaryach - Jesus Never Claimed to Be God)

#### Step 1: Note That Jesus Never Explicitly Said "I Am God"

Argue that if Jesus were God, he would have clearly stated, "I am God" or "I am Yahweh." Instead, he consistently called himself the "Son of God," a title of submission and office. When the Jews accused him of making himself God (John 10:33), he did not affirm the charge but redirected the conversation to Psalm 82:6.

#### Step 2: Explain John 10:34-36 as a Denial

Argue that Jesus' response in John 10:34-36 is a denial, not an affirmation. He says, "Is it not written in your law, I said, Ye are gods?" If he were claiming to be God, he would have said so directly. Instead, he used Psalm 82 to show that the term "god" can be applied to those to whom God's word comes, thereby deflecting the charge of blasphemy.

#### Step 3: Maintain That Matthew 19:17 Is a Denial of Deity

Argue that Jesus' statement, "Why callest thou me good? there is none good but one, that is God," is a clear denial that he is God or even good in himself. If he were God, he would have said, "I am good, for I am God." Instead, he pointed the young man to the Father as the only good one.

#### Step 4: Argue That Jesus' Submission to the Father Proves He Is Not God

Argue that Jesus' prayers, his obedience, his baptism where the Father speaks from heaven, and his Gethsemane prayer ("Not my will, but thine, be done") all demonstrate a relationship of submission to a higher authority. A God does not report to, obey, or have a God over him. Jesus' own words and actions prove he is not the Most High God.
