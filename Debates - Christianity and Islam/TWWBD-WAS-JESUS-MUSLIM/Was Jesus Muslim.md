# WAS JESUS MUSLIM?

## Overview

This debate examines whether Jesus of Nazareth can be considered a Muslim according to Islamic theology. A Christian apologist and Dr. Nasser (Muslim scholar) argue about the definition of "Muslim," the theological content of Jesus' teachings, and whether Jesus' self-understanding and claims align with Islamic conceptions of prophethood. The discussion centers on biblical texts, Quranic verses, and the proper use of theological terminology such as "Father," "Son of God," and "rock" when applied to God.

## When to Follow Debate

- When analyzing interfaith claims about Jesus' religious identity
- When examining how theological terminology functions across religions
- When evaluating whether biblical depictions of Jesus are compatible with Islamic theology
- When discussing the definition of "Muslim" as "one who submits to God"

## Glossary / Prerequisite Terms To Know Before Following Debate

- **Muslim**: One who submits to the will of God (Islam). In this debate, specifically one who submits to the God of Islam as described in the Quran and Hadith, affirming the oneness of God, the prophethood of Muhammad, and rejecting any literal divine sonship or trinitarian theology.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: Submission to Allah alone, recognizing all prophets including Muhammad, and using terminology aligned with the 99 names of Allah.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: The term cannot be applied to Jesus because Jesus affirmed a multi-personal God and claimed unique divine sonship, which contradicts Islamic theology.
- **Allah**: The Arabic word for God, specifically the God of Islam. In this debate, the all-powerful creator of the universe who is one, not begotten, and has no children in any literal or theological sense.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: The singular, transcendent creator whose names and attributes are defined in the Quran.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: Refers to the same creator God but contested whether the specific theology of Allah matches the God revealed in Jesus' teachings.
- **Figurative Language**: Terminology applied metaphorically rather than literally, such as calling God a "rock" or "father" to mean foundation or creator.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: Figurative meanings are acceptable only if they align with the names and attributes of Allah. The terminology itself may be rejected if it carries incorrect theological connotations.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: Figurative language in the Bible (e.g., " Yahweh is my rock") reveals true theological meaning that can be affirmed across traditions.
- **Theology**: The study of the nature of God and religious belief. In this debate, specifically the content of beliefs about God's identity, attributes, and relationship to humanity.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: The specific theology of God as absolutely one, non-physical, without offspring, and unlike his creation.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: The theology of God as Trinity, with Jesus as the unique divine Son, which the Christian argues Jesus himself taught.
- **Prophet**: A person chosen by God to convey divine revelation. In Islam, prophets include Abraham, Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad, all of whom taught the same core message of monotheism.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: Jesus was a prophet who called people to submit to the one God, worship Allah alone, and follow the pure monotheism later perfected in Islam.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: Jesus was not merely a prophet but the unique Son of God, and his teachings about God's fatherhood and his own identity are incompatible with Islam.
- **Son of God**: A title with contested meaning. In Christianity, it refers to Jesus' unique divine nature and relationship to the Father. In Islam, it is rejected as literal and understood as a corrupted term.
  - From Muslim Viewpoint: A term misused by Christians to imply God has a physical offspring. Any figurative use (e.g., beloved of God) is acceptable only if stripped of literal connotations.
  - From Christian Viewpoint: Affirms Jesus' unique divine status and his distinct relationship with the Father, which Jesus taught and which the gospels record.

## Discussed Topics

### Topic 1 / Definition of Muslim

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Linguistic Definition

Dr. Nasser defines a Muslim as "one who submits to God Almighty." He clarifies that this specifically means submitting to the God of Islam as described in the Quran and Hadith, not merely any concept of God. The Christian interlocutor challenges this by noting that many people believe they submit to God but would not be considered Muslim by Dr. Nasser's standards.

##### Example 2: Historical Application

Dr. Nasser explains that at the time of Jesus, submission involved accepting the prophet sent at that time. He argues that Jesus, like Moses and Abraham, taught the same message: belief in the one all-powerful creator. Therefore, Jesus was a Muslim in the sense that he submitted to God and taught monotheism.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Define terms explicitly before applying them to historical figures
- ✅ Clarify whether terminology is being used literally or figuratively
- ✅ Identify the specific theological content of a term, not just its general meaning
- ❌ Avoid equivocation: do not use a broad definition of "submission to God" to include contradictory theologies
- ❌ Avoid anachronism: do not project modern religious identities onto ancient figures without justification

#### Keep In Mind

- The debate hinges on whether "Muslim" is defined broadly (anyone who submits to one God) or narrowly (one who accepts the specific theology and prophethood of Muhammad).
- Dr. Nasser repeatedly emphasizes that terminology must be understood in its cultural and theological context.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Discussions of religious identity can be sensitive. Maintain respect for differing viewpoints.
- Avoid making definitive truth claims about another person's salvation or eternal destiny based on debated terminology.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Confusing linguistic definitions with theological definitions. A person may linguistically "submit to God" but hold a theology incompatible with Islam.
  **Solution:** Always specify the theology associated with the term, not just the general concept.

### Topic 1 / Christian View

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Theological Incompatibility

The Christian argues that Jesus affirmed a multi-personal God (Trinity), called God "Father" in a unique sense, and claimed to be the unique Son of God. These teachings directly contradict Islamic theology, which affirms God as strictly one, non-physical, without offspring. Therefore, Jesus cannot be a Muslim.

##### Example 2: Biblical Evidence

The Christian cites Mark 12:29 ("Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one") to show Jesus taught monotheism, but argues this is compatible with neither Islam nor Christianity alone. He also cites Matthew 21's parable of the tenants, where Jesus distinguishes himself as "the son" of the vineyard owner (God) from the prophets (servants), claiming this demonstrates Jesus' unique self-understanding incompatible with Islam.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Ground claims in specific biblical texts and their immediate context
- ✅ Distinguish between what Jesus taught about God and what Jesus claimed about himself
- ✅ Acknowledge the historical context of Jesus' Jewish audience and terminology
- ❌ Do not assert that Jesus explicitly taught the Trinity in so many words; this requires theological inference
- ❌ Do not dismiss the Jewish context of Jesus' language as irrelevant

#### Keep In Mind

- The Christian view depends on reading the gospels as historically reliable and theologically normative.
- The debate over whether Jesus claimed to be the Son of God is central; the Christian affirms he did, the Muslim denies this or reinterprets it.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Claims about Jesus' deity are deeply sensitive in interfaith dialogue.
- Be aware that asserting Jesus is not a Muslim may be perceived as dismissive of Islamic belief.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming the gospel passages cited are unambiguous about Jesus' deity. The Muslim interlocutor argues that the high Christology reflects later church development, not Jesus' own teaching.
  **Solution:** Engage with historical Jesus scholarship and the distinction between early and late biblical texts.

### Topic 2 / Terminology: "Father" and "Rock"

#### Examples

##### Example 1: The "Rock" Analogy

Dr. Nasser asks whether the Christian would reject Psalm 18's "Yahweh, you are my rock." The Christian affirms it figuratively as foundation. Dr. Nasser argues that if a word can be understood figuratively in line with Islamic theology, the terminology itself can be rejected while affirming the meaning. He applies this to "Father": reject the word, but affirm meanings like creator and sustainer if they align with Allah's attributes.

##### Example 2: The "Father" Debate

The debate extensively circles whether a Muslim can affirm Allah as "Father" in any sense. Dr. Nasser initially refuses to adopt the term but affirms the underlying meanings (creator, sustainer, loving). The Christian presses for a yes/no answer. Dr. Nasser eventually says that in the time of the Israelites, such terminology was used culturally with meanings compatible with Islam, but today the term is rejected because of its corrupted theological associations.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between terminology and meaning: words carry historical and theological baggage
- ✅ Understand cultural context: ancient Israelite use of "father" differs from later Christian Trinitarian use
- ✅ Clarify whether a term is being used literally or figuratively before affirming or rejecting it
- ❌ Avoid conflating different historical uses of the same word
- ❌ Do not demand yes/no answers when theological nuance requires explanation

#### Keep In Mind

- Dr. Nasser's position is that he does not need to adopt foreign terminology to affirm correct theology.
- The Christian argues that if the meaning is affirmed, the terminology should also be acceptable, revealing an underlying theological disagreement about God's nature.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Debates about God's "fatherhood" touch on core Islamic and Christian identities.
- Muslims reject any literal or permanent attribution of "father" to Allah, while Christians see this as central to Jesus' revelation.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Demanding a simple yes/no to complex theological questions. Dr. Nasser repeatedly refuses to answer "Is Allah a father in any sense?" with a yes or no, arguing the question requires unpacking the meaning.
  **Solution:** Allow for nuanced answers that distinguish between literal and figurative, permanent and cultural.

### Topic 3 / Quranic Arguments: Surah 5:18 and Surah 19

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Surah 5:18

The Christian cites Quran 5:18: "The Jews and Christians say, 'We are the sons of Allah and His loved ones.' Say, 'Then why does He punish you for your sins?'" He argues this verse condemns the very terminology of sonship and belovedness because it implies God would not punish those He loves. Dr. Nasser counters that the verse is not about the terminology itself but about arrogance: claiming special status while continuing in sin. He notes that the Quran elsewhere affirms Allah loves believers (e.g., those who are patient, rely on God).

##### Example 2: Surah 19 (Maryam)

The Christian references Surah 19, verses 3 and 93, which he claims affirm that no one can come to Allah except as a servant. He argues this contradicts Jesus' teaching that believers are children of God. Dr. Nasser responds that Allah has many categories of people He loves, and that "son of God" terminology was rejected due to its association with literal divine offspring claims, not because righteous people cannot be beloved.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Read Quranic verses in their immediate and broader context
- ✅ Identify the specific rhetorical situation of the verse (to whom was it revealed, what error was it correcting?)
- ✅ Note the difference between rejecting terminology and rejecting the underlying meaning
- ❌ Do not isolate verses from their context to force a contradiction
- ❌ Do not assume the Quran teaches the same theology as the Bible or vice versa

#### Keep In Mind

- The Muslim position is that the Quran corrects corrupted theology, not that it rejects all good meanings.
- The Christian position is that the Quran contradicts Jesus' own teachings about God's fatherhood and human status as beloved children.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Quranic interpretation is highly sensitive. Muslims may view proof-texting without scholarly context as disrespectful or misleading.
- Acknowledge that both sides claim their interpretation is correct.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Using a verse out of context to claim the opposite religion teaches error. The Christian uses 5:18 to argue Islam rejects belovedness to God; Dr. Nasser shows this is a misreading.
  **Solution:** Always examine the surrounding verses and classical tafsir (exegesis) before drawing conclusions.

### Topic 4 / Historical Jesus and Biblical Reliability

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Low vs. High Christology

Dr. Nasser argues that earlier biblical sources reflect lower Christology (Jesus as Jewish prophet) while later sources reflect higher Christology (Jesus as divine Son). He suggests the historical Jesus aligns with Islam. The Christian counters that if the gospel accounts are our best historical source, and those accounts record Jesus calling God "Father" uniquely and claiming sonship, then the historical Jesus held these beliefs.

##### Example 2: Matthew 21 Parable

Dr. Nasser cites Matthew 21, where after the parable of the tenants, the crowd concludes Jesus is a prophet. He argues this shows Jesus was understood as a prophet, not divine Son. The Christian agrees Jesus was a prophet but argues the parable itself shows Jesus distinguishing himself as "the son" of God, unique from other prophets.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Distinguish between what Jesus said, what the gospels record, and what later theology derived from those records
- ✅ Engage with basic historical-critical scholarship on gospel development
- ✅ Acknowledge the limits of historical knowledge about a 2,000-year-old figure
- ❌ Do not assert that the gospels are either wholly reliable or wholly unreliable without argument
- ❌ Do not conflate the historical Jesus with the theological claims made about him in later Christianity

#### Keep In Mind

- The Christian accepts the Bible as authoritative; the Muslim does not, which means they operate with different evidentiary standards.
- Dr. Nasser's position relies on a critical reading of biblical development, while the Christian position relies on the final form of the text.

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Questions of biblical reliability are central to Christian identity; handle with care.
- Muslims may view the Bible as corrupted; Christians may view the Quran as human invention. These foundational commitments cannot be resolved within the debate itself.

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Assuming the gospels provide a transparent, uninterpreted window into Jesus' self-understanding.
  **Solution:** Recognize that the gospels are theological documents written by communities with beliefs about Jesus.

## Steps For Each Topic and Each Topic's View

### Topic 1 / Definition of Muslim

#### Step 1: Define the term explicitly

Establish what "Muslim" means in general (submission to God) and in specific Islamic theology (submission to Allah as revealed in the Quran, affirming Muhammad's prophethood, rejecting tritheism, literal sonship, and idolatry). Do not assume the definition is shared by all parties.

#### Step 2: Identify the criteria for membership

Ask: What must a person believe to be a Muslim? Is it merely monotheism and moral behavior, or must they accept the specific message of Muhammad and the Quran's description of God? Dr. Nasser argues for the latter; the Christian argues the former is too broad.

#### Step 3: Apply criteria to Jesus

Given the agreed definition, evaluate whether Jesus meets the criteria. The Christian says no because Jesus' theology contradicts Islamic theology. The Muslim says yes because Jesus taught monotheism, prayed to the one God, and was a prophet. The disagreement depends on whether Jesus' own claims about himself are compatible with Islamic theology.

### Topic 1 / Christian View

#### Step 1: Present Jesus' self-understanding from the gospels

Cite passages where Jesus distinguishes himself from other prophets (e.g., Matthew 21's parable where he is "the son" distinct from "the servants"), where he prays to the Father (John 17), and where he claims unique authority (forgiveness of sins, authority over all flesh).

#### Step 2: Demonstrate theological incompatibility

Argue that Jesus' teaching about God as Father and himself as Son cannot be reduced to monotheistic prophecy. If Jesus is the unique Son of God in any meaningful sense, he cannot be a Muslim because Islam rejects divine sonship.

#### Step 3: Conclude impossibility

Given that the Bible is the best historical source for Jesus' life and teachings, and given that Jesus taught a theology incompatible with Islam, conclude that Jesus was not a Muslim.

### Topic 2 / Terminology: "Father" and "Rock"

#### Step 1: Establish the meaning of terms in context

Examine ancient Israelite usage of "father" and "rock" in the Old Testament (e.g., Psalm 18, Deuteronomy 32, Isaiah 63). Determine whether these terms were understood literally or figuratively, and what theological meanings they carried.

#### Step 2: Determine whether meanings are compatible with Islamic theology

For each term, ask: Does the underlying meaning (e.g., God as foundation, creator, sustainer) align with the names and attributes of Allah? Dr. Nasser says yes for "rock" (as foundation) and for "father" (as creator/sustainer), but he rejects the terminology due to its association with incorrect theology.

#### Step 3: Decide on terminology adoption

Ask whether the Muslim is obligated to adopt the terminology of another culture or may affirm the meaning while rejecting the word. Dr. Nasser argues he may affirm the meaning without adopting the word. The Christian argues this is inconsistent and reveals an underlying theological disagreement.

#### Step 4: Apply to Jesus

If Jesus used "Father" in a way compatible with Islam (i.e., as creator/sustainer, not literal offspring), then his use is acceptable. If he used it in a way incompatible with Islam (i.e., claiming unique divine sonship), then he was not a Muslim. The debate hinges on the historical meaning of Jesus' usage.

### Topic 2 / Christian View

#### Step 1: Challenge the Muslim's refusal to adopt terminology

Argue that if the meaning is affirmed, the word should also be acceptable. If God is truly a father in the sense of creator and sustainer, why refuse the term? This suggests the real objection is not the word but the theology.

#### Step 2: Expose the underlying theological disagreement

Point out that the Muslim's real objection is to any language that could imply God has offspring or is multi-personal. Therefore, the debate is not about words but about the nature of God: Is God a Trinity? Is Jesus the unique Son? The Christian affirms yes; the Muslim denies it.

#### Step 3: Apply to Jesus' prayer

Cite Jesus' prayers in the gospels (e.g., John 17, Matthew 26:39) where he addresses God as "Father" and asks to be glorified. Ask: Can a Muslim pray this way? The Christian argues no, because it implies a unique filial relationship incompatible with Islam. The Muslim argues yes, in the Jewish cultural context, as long as the meaning is not literal offspring.

### Topic 3 / Quranic Arguments: Surah 5:18 and Surah 19

#### Step 1: Read the verse in context

Read Quran 5:18 in its surrounding context (Surah Al-Ma'idah, verses 15-21). Note that it addresses Jews and Christians who claim special status as "sons of Allah and His loved ones" while persisting in sin. The rebuke is against self-righteousness and presumption, not against being beloved to God per se.

#### Step 2: Identify the theological error being corrected

Ask: What specific belief or behavior is the Quran correcting? Dr. Nasser argues it is the arrogance of assuming guaranteed salvation by virtue of religious identity. The Christian argues it is the terminology itself, implying that calling oneself a "son of God" is wrong.

#### Step 3: Cross-reference with other Quranic verses

Note that the Quran elsewhere affirms Allah loves those who are patient, rely on Him, and are righteous. Therefore, the Quran does not reject the concept of being beloved to God, only the arrogant claim of automatic belovedness. This shows the verse is not about terminology but about attitude.

#### Step 4: Apply to Jesus

If Jesus, as a righteous prophet, could be considered beloved to God, then there is no contradiction between his teaching and the Quran. The Christian argues Jesus' teaching goes further: he assures his followers of their beloved status in a way the Quran does not.

### Topic 3 / Christian View

#### Step 1: Argue the verse condemns the terminology

Read Quran 5:18 as a direct rejection of "sons of Allah" language. Note that the Quran says, "Why does He punish you for your sins?" implying that if they were truly sons and beloved, God would not punish them. This contradicts the biblical teaching that God disciplines His children (Hebrews 12, Deuteronomy 8).

#### Step 2: Cross-reference with biblical theology

Cite Deuteronomy 8:5 ("As a man disciplines his son, so the Lord your God disciplines you") and Hebrews 12 to show that in biblical theology, God disciplines His children. This is consistent with Jesus' teaching that God is a loving Father who cares for His children. The Quran's implicit rejection of this theology shows incompatibility with Jesus' message.

#### Step 3: Conclude theological contradiction

Since Jesus taught that believers are beloved children of God, and the Quran rejects the very idea of being sons/children of God, Jesus' theology contradicts the Quran. Therefore, Jesus cannot be a Muslim.

### Topic 4 / Historical Jesus and Biblical Reliability

#### Step 1: Establish the source for the historical Jesus

Ask: Where do we learn about Jesus? The Christian says the Bible, particularly the gospels. Dr. Nasser says we can reconstruct a historical Jesus from the earliest sources, which show lower Christology. Both agree the Bible is our best source, but they disagree on how to read it.

#### Step 2: Evaluate the historical reliability of gospel claims

The Christian argues the gospels are reliable historical documents. Dr. Nasser suggests the high Christology (Jesus as divine Son) is a later development. The debate touches on the "Q" source, early vs. late gospel material, and the conclusions of historical Jesus scholarship.

#### Step 3: Determine Jesus' self-understanding

Given the historical evidence, ask: Did Jesus see himself as merely a prophet, or as the unique Son of God? The Christian says the evidence shows the latter. The Muslim says the evidence, properly understood, shows the former. The Christian cites Matthew 21's parable where Jesus distinguishes himself as "the son" from the prophets; the Muslim says the crowd concluded he was a prophet, so they understood him correctly.

#### Step 4: Conclude on religious identity

If Jesus was a prophet only, he could be a Muslim. If he claimed unique divine sonship, he cannot be. The debate ends without resolution, with each side claiming the evidence supports their view.

### Topic 4 / Christian View

#### Step 1: Affirm biblical reliability

Argue that the gospels, while theological, are based on eyewitness testimony and reliably transmit Jesus' teachings. The high Christology is not a late addition but reflects Jesus' own self-understanding.

#### Step 2: Cite Jesus' claims to unique authority

Point to Jesus' authority to forgive sins (Mark 2), his claim to be the unique Son of God (Mark 12:6, John 5:18), and his teaching about the kingdom of God as his own message, not merely a continuation of Judaism.

#### Step 3: Conclude Jesus was not a Muslim

Given that Jesus claimed a unique relationship with God as Son, taught a theology of God's fatherhood that includes believers as beloved children, and was worshiped as divine by his followers, he cannot be considered a Muslim. A Muslim, by definition, rejects these teachings.
