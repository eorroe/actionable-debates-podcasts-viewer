# 1 Christian vs. 12 Muslim Women ｜ The Bridge

## Overview

A cross-faith dialogue between one Christian woman and twelve Muslim women examining five theological and moral prompts addressing core doctrinal claims, as characterized by the host Dr. Dab: the biblical basis of the Trinity, the moral example of Muhammad, whether Allah is God, the Quran's divine origin and preservation, and the treatment of women in Islam. Each prompt is debated from both Christian and Muslim perspectives, drawing on scripture, Hadith, historical context, and personal experience. The conversation aims to address disagreements respectfully while highlighting identified common ground, as identified on the Dr. Dab Show, between the two faith traditions. All claims, arguments, and statistics in this debate summary are drawn from the Dr. Dab Show video '1 Christian vs. 12 Muslim Women | The Bridge' and reflect the positions presented by the participants in that specific recording.

## Discussed Topics

### Topic 1 / View 1: The Trinity Is Biblical (Christian Perspective)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Nehemiah 9:6

Christina argues that Nehemiah 9:6 identifies Yahweh as God's personal name, yet the Father is called Yahweh in Isaiah 43:10-11, the Son is identified with the divine name in Isaiah 9:6 and Psalm 45:6-7 (quoted of Christ in Hebrews 1:8), and the Holy Spirit is identified with Yahweh in Acts 5:3-4. She asks whether "you alone" in Nehemiah 9:6 refers to the divine essence or one person of the Trinity. The Muslim panelists respond that the term God is not univocal and that Nehemiah 9:6 addresses God's personal name, which should not be borne by anyone else.

##### Example 2: Matthew 28:19

Christina cites the Great Commission in Matthew 28:19 where Jesus commands baptism in the singular name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. She asks whether Muslims can perform zakat in the name of Allah, Muhammad, and Jibril. The panelists discuss how church fathers quote the verse, with Christian panelists noting that Eusebius quotes Matthew 28:19 both ways, according to the Dr. Dab Show debate

##### Example 3: The Origin of the Word "Trinity"

A Muslim panelist states Christian historians, such as those cited on the Dr. Dab Show, argue that the formal terminology of the Trinity was developed at the Council of Nicaea under Constantine, arguing that the Roman emperor cult—including the worship of Sol Invictus (the Unconquered Sun) and the adoption of dies Solis (Sunday) as the primary day of worship—may have influenced the doctrine, according to historians. Christina redirects to scripture as the source of the debate, emphasizing that the focus should be on what the Bible teaches rather than the origin of the word.

##### Example 4: The Begotten Son

The discussion turns to Psalm 2:7 and the meaning of "begotten." Christina explains monogenes means "unique," not biological generation. The Christian panelists, including Christina, argue that Adam would also qualify as a "son" of God, according to the Dr. Dab Show debate, and question whether Jesus is uniquely the Son.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Frame questions around scripture rather than philosophical or historical origin arguments
- ✅ Acknowledge shared monotheistic roots before exploring differences
- ✅ Distinguish between addressing God's essence versus His persons when interpreting biblical texts
- ✅ Clarify the meaning of key terms like "Trinity," "Son of God," and "Yahweh" before assuming shared definitions
- ❌ Avoid dismissing opposing arguments by shifting to unrelated historical or political claims
- ❌ Avoid conflating the word "Trinity" with the underlying biblical concept
- ❌ Avoid appealing solely to church tradition when the prompt asks about scripture
- ❌ Avoid assuming the opponent shares the same definition of "God," "son," or "one"

#### Keep In Mind

- The Trinity is a doctrine Christians derive from passages such as Genesis 1:26, Isaiah 6:3, Isaiah 9:6, Matthew 28:19, and 2 Corinthians 13:14, among other passages; Christians argue there is not a single verse in the Bible that explicitly uses the word "Trinity".
- Muslims affirm strict monotheism (tawhid) and generally view any division or multiplicity in God as shirk (associating partners with Allah)
- The debate is most productive when it focuses on what each side considers authoritative: the Bible for Christians, the Quran and authentic Sunnah for Muslims
- Both Christina and the Muslim panelists acknowledged, according to the Dr. Dab Show debate, that God is not the author of confusion and that His revelation should be understandable

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Theological disagreements over the nature of God can be deeply personal and emotionally charged
- Avoid framing the debate as a situation where one side wins at the expense of the other, as this can create defensive postures
- Be mindful that discussions about the Trinity can be considered blasphemous in certain Islamic contexts, according to the Muslim panelists

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Interpreting "one God" in the Bible as inherently excluding any internal distinctions or persons
  **Solution:** Explore the full biblical context where plural language and distinct persons are attributed to the one God
- **Problem:** Dismissing the Trinity as unbiblical because the exact word does not appear in scripture
  **Solution:** Note that many theological terms (such as "Bible" itself) describe concepts derived from, but not explicitly named in, the texts
- **Problem:** Equating submission or authority hierarchies with inferiority of personhood
  **Solution:** Distinguish between functional subordination and ontological equality, as the debate illustrates with the boss-employee analogy

### Topic 1 / View 2: The Trinity Is Not Biblical (Muslim Perspective)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Tawhid and the Rejection of the Trinity as Shirk

A Muslim panelist presents the Islamic doctrine of tawhid: Allah is absolutely one (ahad), with no partners, no sons, and no multiplicity in His essence. The Trinity is understood as shirk — associating partners with Allah — which is the one unforgivable sin in Islam if one dies upon it without repentance. Cite Quran 112:1-4: "Say, He is Allah, the One. Allah, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, nor is there to Him any equivalent." The panelist asks Christina to consider whether adding two more persons to the Godhead violates the clear declaration of God's oneness.

##### Example 2: The Shema and the Declaration of God's Oneness

Cite Deuteronomy 6:4: "Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one." A Muslim panelist argues that this foundational declaration, affirmed by both Judaism and Christianity as the central statement of faith, affirms strict monotheism, not a triune God. She asks, according to the Dr. Dab Show debate, whether the word "one" (echad in Hebrew) has been consistently understood as singular throughout Jewish and Christian history, or whether it has been reinterpreted to fit a later doctrinal formula.

##### Example 3: The Historical Origin of the Trinity Doctrine

A Muslim panelist argues that the word "Trinity" and the formal doctrine of three co-eternal persons originated at the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE, centuries after Jesus. She notes that Emperor Constantine, who convened the council, was a former pagan who patronized the Roman emperor cult including the worship of Sol Invictus (the Unconquered Sun), and that Christian scholars, according to the Dr. Dab Show debate, argue the doctrine reflects influences from Greek philosophical systems including Platonic tripartite structures and Neoplatonic emanations. Christina redirects to scripture as the source of the debate, but the panelist maintains that the absence of the doctrine in the earliest centuries of Christianity undermines its claim to be apostolic teaching.

##### Example 4: Internal Christian Disagreement About the Trinity

A Muslim panelist notes that not all Christian groups affirm the Trinity as defined at Nicaea: Unitarian Christians reject it entirely, Jehovah's Witnesses believe Jesus is a created being, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints believes in a corporeal God the Father, Oneness Pentecostals reject distinct persons in the Godhead, and Seventh-day Adventists hold a nuanced view emphasizing Christ's ministry over co-eternity. She argues this diversity suggests to some that the Trinity is a human doctrinal construct, not a clear and unambiguous biblical teaching.

##### Example 5: The Quran's Direct Rejection of the Trinity

The panelist cites Quran 4:171: "Do not say 'Three' - desist! It will be better for you." She argues that the Quran explicitly and repeatedly affirms the oneness of God in clear and accessible language, directly addressing what the Muslim panelists considered the Trinitarian formula. She asks Christina to explain how a doctrine that the Quran explicitly condemns can be considered biblical.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Frame the Muslim argument around Tawhid as the central, non-negotiable doctrine of Islam, not merely "monotheism" as a generic concept
- ✅ Acknowledge shared Abrahamic roots before explaining where the Trinity diverges from strict monotheism
- ✅ Distinguish between the Christian concept of "one essence in three persons" and the Islamic concept of "one without any partners, equals, or divisions"
- ✅ Clarify that the Muslim rejection of the Trinity is rooted in the concept of shirk, the one unforgivable sin in Islamic theology
- ❌ Avoid reducing Tawhid to simple "monotheism" without engaging its deep philosophical and theological dimensions in Islamic thought
- ❌ Avoid conflating the Islamic concept of Allah's unity with the Christian concept of God's oneness as if they were the same
- ❌ Avoid dismissing the Muslim concern about shirk without explaining why Christians believe the Trinity does not constitute associating partners with God
- ❌ Avoid appealing solely to church tradition or councils when the Muslim perspective asks for explicit biblical evidence

#### Keep In Mind

- Tawhid is the foundational and non-negotiable doctrine of Islam; any suggestion of multiplicity, division, or partnership in God is considered shirk by Muslims
- The Muslim rejection of the Trinity, the Muslim panelists argued, is not merely a doctrinal disagreement but a moral and spiritual one — it touches on the core of Islamic faith
- The debate hinges on how each tradition reads the same biblical texts: Christians see Trinitarian patterns, Muslims see strict monotheism
- Both traditions claim to worship the God of Abraham, but understand the nature of that God's oneness in fundamentally different ways

#### Security & Safety Notes

- In certain Islamic contexts, openly rejecting Tawhid or embracing the Trinity can carry serious social, legal, or even physical consequences
- Muslims who question or leave Islam over the Trinity doctrine may face ostracism or danger in some communities.
- Christians engaging on this topic should be aware that the concept of the Trinity can be deeply offensive to many Muslims and approach the discussion with genuine respect

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Citing verses like Matthew 28:19 without addressing how Muslims interpret the same text in light of Tawhid
  **Solution:** Engage directly with the Muslim interpretation of key verses rather than assuming the Christian reading is self-evident
- **Problem:** Dismissing the Muslim rejection of the Trinity as simply "not understanding" Christian theology
  **Solution:** Recognize that the rejection is theologically coherent within the framework of Tawhid and deserves serious engagement
- **Problem:** Treating Tawhid as equivalent to the Christian affirmation of God's oneness without acknowledging the ontological differences
  **Solution:** Clarify that Tawhid means God is one in a way that excludes any real distinction of persons, which is fundamentally different from the Christian understanding

### Topic 2 / View 1: Muhammad Is Not a Good Moral Example for All Mankind (Christian Perspective)

#### Examples

##### Example 1: Aisha's Age at Marriage

Christina raises the issue of Muhammad marrying Aisha when she was six and consummating the marriage when she was nine, citing Sahih Muslim 1422. The panelists challenge the authenticity of the Hadith, the reliability of the chain of narration, and the ability to judge historical figures by modern standards. Historical analyses cited on the Dr. Dab Show suggest she was at least 19 based on historical calculations, while others acknowledge the Hadith but argue for contextual understanding.

##### Example 2: Judging Historical Figures by Modern Standards

A Muslim panelist argues that one cannot judge 7th-century practices by 21st-century standards, citing that Mary was likely between 12-16 when she became pregnant with Jesus (as suggested by historical analyses cited on the Dr. Dab Show), which would also be illegal today. She asks whether Christians should hold biblical figures to the same standard.

##### Example 3: The Bible's Own Moral Challenges

A Christian panelist brings up Deuteronomy 22:28-29 in the Septuagint, arguing that the Bible also contains passages that classical Islamic scholars such as Ibn Hajar classify this as marriage before puberty according to their fiqh rulings (nikah al-saghir), a practice addressed in classical Islamic jurisprudence with specific conditions including the right of the bride to annul the marriage upon reaching puberty (bulugh). This is used to argue that Christians applying a double standard when criticizing Muhammad.

##### Example 4: Evolutionary Biology and Puberty

Christina cites demographic studies cited on the Dr. Dab Show suggesting that around the time of Muhammad, only 1% of girls had their period by age 11, with the average being 14. She argues that marriage at age six and consummation at nine would have been abnormal even then, challenging the argument that it was culturally typical.

##### Example 5: The Game of Telephone

Several panelists, including Sawa and Miriam, compare Hadith transmission to a sequential transmission model analogous to the children's game Chinese whispers. Muslim scholars note that Hadith transmission also includes rigorous written records and isnad criticism. In this model, a message changes as it passes through multiple people. Information is passed through multiple intermediaries, and cumulative distortions accumulate at each step. They argue that trusting specific narrations about Aisha's age is unreliable compared to the Quran's direct revelation.

#### Best Practices

- ✅ Evaluate historical claims using multiple sources rather than relying on a single Hadith or narration
- ✅ Distinguish between what is directly in the Quran versus what is in Hadith collections
- ✅ Consider cultural, historical, and linguistic context when assessing moral claims about ancient figures
- ✅ Apply consistent moral standards across all religious traditions when making comparative arguments
- ❌ Avoid using inflammatory or "gotcha" prompts without genuine intent to understand the other perspective
- ❌ Avoid dismissing all Hadith as unreliable simply because some are questioned
- ❌ Avoid judging ancient cultures solely through a modern secular ethical lens without acknowledging the difference in context
- ❌ Avoid cherry-picking difficult passages from one scripture while ignoring similar challenges in your own

#### Keep In Mind

- The age of consent and marriage has varied dramatically throughout history and across cultures
- What is considered "moral" can be influenced by both divine revelation and cultural context
- Both the Bible and the Quran contain passages that, when read literally or out of context, can raise moral questions, according to the discussion
- Personal identity and emotional investment in a tradition can make these discussions particularly sensitive

#### Security & Safety Notes

- Discussions about the Prophet Muhammad's personal life can be deeply offensive to many Muslims and are considered blasphemous in certain jurisdictions
- Discussions about child marriage can trigger trauma in listeners; handle with care and avoid graphic detail
- Interfaith dialogue on these topics should be conducted with clear consent and mutual respect

#### Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Using a single Hadith to condemn the entire Islamic tradition or the character of Muhammad
